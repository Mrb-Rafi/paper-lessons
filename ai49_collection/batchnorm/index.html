Batch Normalization,
from zero.

In 2015 two researchers at Google added one small step inside a neural network: before each layer bends its numbers, re-centre them to mean 0 and rescale them to spread 1, using the examples in the current minibatch, then let the network re-stretch them as it likes. Networks trained with this step reached the same accuracy in 14 times fewer training steps, tolerated learning rates 30 times larger, trained with sigmoids where they used to stay stuck, and beat the best ImageNet result. This book starts from “what is a neuron?” and ends with every line of both algorithms, every chain-rule equation, the scale-invariance argument, convolutional batch normalization, and every experiment in the paper. You need no background: every idea comes with something to play with.

Based on Sergey Ioffe and Christian Szegedy, “Batch Normalization: Accelerating Deep Network Training by Reducing Internal Covariate Shift”, ICML 2015 (arXiv:1502.03167). The paper has no official code release; the book follows Algorithms 1 and 2 themselves and the reference implementation in PyTorch’s torch/nn/modules/batchnorm.py (v2.5.0). This book is an independent educational companion, not an official publication of the authors.

Foundations

Neurons, layers and deep networks

A deep network is a stack of layers, and each layer works on whatever the layer below hands it.

A neuron takes a few numbers, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up together with a bias, and passes the total through a bending function, the activation function (or nonlinearity). Try it:

Put many neurons side by side and they form a layer. The layer’s inputs are a list of numbers, a vector \(u\); its weights form a table, a matrix \(W\), with one column per neuron; its biases form a vector \(b\). Stack layers so that each one’s outputs are the next one’s inputs, and you have a neural network. The layers in the middle are hidden layers; a network with many of them is deep.

∑ The math (paper §1, §3.2)

The paper writes one layer as

\[ z = g(Wu + b) \]

\(u\) is the layer’s input vector, \(W\) its weight matrix, \(b\) its bias vector, \(Wu\) means “multiply the matrix by the vector” (each output entry is a weighted sum of all inputs), and \(g\) is the nonlinearity applied to every entry separately, for example the sigmoid \(g(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})\) or the ReLU \(\max(x, 0)\). \(z\) is the layer’s output. The quantity inside the bend, \(x = Wu + b\), is what Batch Normalization will act on, so it gets its own name: the pre-activation. Tiny example: \(u = (1, 2)\), one neuron with weights \((0.5, -1)\) and bias \(0.5\): \(x = 0.5\cdot1 - 1\cdot2 + 0.5 = -1\), and a sigmoid gives \(z = 1/(1+e^{1}) \approx 0.27\).

Stacking has a side effect that the whole paper is about. Every layer’s input depends on the weights of all layers below it. Nudge the first layer a little, and the third layer sees a different picture:

📄 In the paper

“The inputs to each layer are affected by the parameters of all preceding layers – so that small changes to the network parameters amplify as the network becomes deeper” Paper §1. The layer form \(z = g(Wu+b)\) appears in §1 (for sigmoids) and §3.2 (“this formulation covers both fully-connected and convolutional layers”).

Foundations

Loss, gradients and minibatch SGD

Training follows the slope of the loss downhill, estimating that slope from a small random minibatch of examples.

To train a network we need a number that says how wrong it is: the loss \(\ell\). For classification it is usually the cross-entropy: minus the log of the probability the network gave to the right answer. The network’s weights and biases, all collected together, are its parameters \(\Theta\) (capital theta). Training searches for the \(\Theta\) that makes the average loss small.

The gradient of the loss tells, for every parameter, how much the loss would rise if that parameter grew a little. Gradient descent steps every parameter a little against its gradient. The size of the step is the learning rate \(\alpha\).

With a million training examples, computing the exact gradient for every step is slow. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) estimates it from a random handful of \(m\) examples, a minibatch. The estimate is noisy, but right on average, and it gets better as \(m\) grows:

∑ The math (paper §1)
\[ \Theta = \arg\min_{\Theta} \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell(x_i, \Theta), \qquad \text{gradient estimate: } \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{\partial \ell(x_i, \Theta)}{\partial \Theta}. \]

\(x_1 \dots x_N\) are the \(N\) training examples, \(\ell(x_i, \Theta)\) is the loss on example \(i\), and \(\arg\min_\Theta\) means “the \(\Theta\) that makes this smallest”. \(\partial \ell/\partial\Theta\) is the gradient (the symbol \(\partial\) reads “partial”: change one parameter at a time). On the right, the sum runs over the \(m\) examples of the current minibatch only. Worked example: if three examples give gradients 0.2, 0.5 and −0.1 for one weight, the minibatch estimate is \((0.2 + 0.5 - 0.1)/3 = 0.2\).

📄 In the paper

The paper names two benefits of minibatches: “the gradient of the loss over a mini-batch is an estimate of the gradient over the training set, whose quality improves as the batch size increases”, and computing on a batch is more efficient than \(m\) separate computations on modern hardware Paper §1. It also names the catch: SGD “requires careful tuning of the model hyper-parameters, specifically the learning rate and the initial parameter values”. BN will use the minibatch for a second purpose: to measure statistics.

Foundations

Backpropagation and the chain rule

Gradients flow backwards through a computation, multiplied by one small local slope at each step.

A network computes its loss in many small steps. To get the gradient of the loss with respect to something deep inside, we use the chain rule: if \(\ell\) depends on \(y\), and \(y\) depends on \(x\), then a small change in \(x\) changes \(\ell\) by (how much \(y\) moves per unit of \(x\)) × (how much \(\ell\) moves per unit of \(y\)). Doing this from the loss backwards through every step is backpropagation.

There is one more rule we will need for Batch Normalization: when a quantity is used in several places, its gradient is the sum of the gradients arriving from each place. Here \(c\) is used twice:

∑ The math
\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}, \qquad \text{and if } x \text{ feeds } y_1, y_2, \dots:\quad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial x} = \sum_j \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial y_j}\cdot\frac{\partial y_j}{\partial x}. \]

\(\partial \ell/\partial y\) is the gradient that arrives from above (“upstream”); \(\partial y/\partial x\) is the local slope of the step from \(x\) to \(y\). Worked example: \(y = 3x\), \(\ell = y^2\), at \(x = 1\): \(\partial\ell/\partial y = 2y = 6\), \(\partial y/\partial x = 3\), so \(\partial\ell/\partial x = 18\). Check: \(\ell = 9x^2\), whose slope at 1 is 18. ✓

The paper’s backpropagation through Batch Normalization (Chapter 18) is nothing but these two rules, applied to the four lines of Algorithm 1. The mean \(\mu\) is used by every example in the batch, so its gradient is a sum over the batch.

⌨ In the code

This collection’s shared/lib/nn.js implements backpropagation for matrices: every operation stores a small _back function with its local slopes, and loss.backward() calls them from the loss downwards, adding up gradients where a value is used more than once.

Foundations

Training a network and the learning rate

The learning rate is the most delicate knob: too small and training crawls, too large and it explodes.

Here is a whole small network learning to separate two kinds of points, with backpropagation and SGD doing the work. Change the learning rate and watch how training behaves.

Why does too large a learning rate explode? Take the simplest possible loss, a bowl \(\ell(w) = \tfrac{1}{2} k\,w^2\) whose steepness is set by the curvature \(k\). One gradient step multiplies \(w\) by \((1 - \alpha k)\). If that factor is bigger than 1 in size, every step makes things worse:

∑ The math
\[ w_{t+1} = w_t - \alpha\,k\,w_t = (1 - \alpha k)\,w_t \quad\Rightarrow\quad w_t = (1-\alpha k)^t\, w_0. \]

\(w_t\) is the weight after \(t\) steps. If \(0 < \alpha k < 1\) it shrinks smoothly; if \(1 < \alpha k < 2\) it overshoots and flips sign each step but still shrinks; if \(\alpha k > 2\), \(|1-\alpha k| > 1\) and it grows without limit: divergence. Worked example: \(k = 4\), \(\alpha = 0.6\): factor \(1 - 2.4 = -1.4\), so after 10 steps \(|w|\) has grown \(1.4^{10} \approx 29\) times.

In a deep network the “curvature” seen by each weight depends on the scale of everything around it. If one layer’s weights grow, the gradients flowing through it grow too, and a learning rate that was safe becomes unsafe. That is why deep networks traditionally needed small learning rates, and it is one of the things BN changes (Chapter 25).

📄 In the paper

“In traditional deep networks, too high a learning rate may result in the gradients that explode or vanish, as well as getting stuck in poor local minima … large learning rates may increase the scale of layer parameters, which then amplify the gradient during backpropagation and lead to the model explosion” Paper §3.3. On ImageNet, raising the original Inception network’s learning rate 5× “caused the model parameters to reach machine infinity” (§4.2.2).

Foundations

Mean, variance and standard deviation

Two numbers summarize where a pile of values sits and how wide it is spread.

The mean \(\mu\) (Greek mu) of some values is their average: where they are centred. The variance \(\sigma^2\) (sigma squared) is the average squared distance from the mean: how spread out they are. Its square root, the standard deviation \(\sigma\), is in the same units as the values. Move the values and watch every intermediate number:

∑ The math (the first two lines of Algorithm 1)
\[ \mu = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} x_i, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} (x_i - \mu)^2. \]

\(x_1 \dots x_m\) are the \(m\) values; \(\sum_{i=1}^m\) means “add up for \(i\) from 1 to \(m\)”. Worked example: values \(1, 2, 3, 6\): \(\mu = 12/4 = 3\); deviations \(-2, -1, 0, 3\); squares \(4, 1, 0, 9\); \(\sigma^2 = 14/4 = 3.5\); \(\sigma \approx 1.87\).

When the values are random draws, statisticians write \(\mathrm{E}[x]\) for the expected value (the mean you would get from infinitely many draws) and \(\mathrm{Var}[x] = \mathrm{E}[(x - \mathrm{E}[x])^2]\) for the true variance. The \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) of a small sample are estimates of them. Chapter 20 shows a subtle bias in the variance estimate that the paper corrects with a factor \(m/(m-1)\).

Foundations

Distributions, histograms and percentiles

A histogram shows the whole shape of a pile of values; three percentiles summarize it in three lines.

A neuron’s pre-activation takes a different value for every input example. The pattern of all those values is its distribution. A histogram draws it: split the number line into bins and count how many values land in each.

A percentile is a cut point: the 15th percentile is the value below which 15% of the values lie. The 50th percentile is the median, the middle value. The paper summarizes a distribution by its 15th, 50th and 85th percentiles; for a bell-shaped (Gaussian or normal) distribution, these sit at about \(\mu - \sigma\), \(\mu\) and \(\mu + \sigma\).

∑ The math

Sort the \(n\) values: \(v_{(1)} \le v_{(2)} \le \dots \le v_{(n)}\). The \(p\)-th percentile is the value at position \(1 + \tfrac{p}{100}(n-1)\) in this list, reading between neighbours when the position is not a whole number. For a normal distribution with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\), the 15th and 85th percentiles are \(\mu \mp 1.04\,\sigma\), because 70% of a normal distribution lies within 1.04 standard deviations of its mean.

📄 In the paper

Figure 1(b, c) shows “the evolution of input distributions to a typical sigmoid, over the course of training, shown as {15, 50, 85}th percentiles” Paper Fig. 1. Three lines per plot: the middle one is the median, and the distance between the outer two is roughly two standard deviations. You will build this plot yourself in Chapter 28.

Foundations

Standardizing a feature

Subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation, and any feature has mean 0 and spread 1.

Data comes in arbitrary units: house sizes in square metres (around 100), numbers of rooms (around 4). Standardizing a feature puts it in common units: subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation. The result, sometimes called a z-score, says “how many standard deviations above average” each value is. Whatever you do to the raw data on the left, the right side stays centred at 0 with spread 1:

∑ The math (paper §3)
\[ \widehat{x} = \frac{x - \mathrm{E}[x]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x]}} \]

\(\widehat{x}\) (“x hat”) is the standardized value, \(\mathrm{E}[x]\) the mean and \(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x]}\) the standard deviation of the feature. Worked example: a feature with mean 100 and variance 400 (standard deviation 20); the value 130 becomes \((130 - 100)/20 = 1.5\).

Why bother? Because gradient descent likes it. When one input is in the hundreds and another in single digits, the loss surface is a long narrow valley: one direction is very steep and the other very flat, and no single learning rate suits both. Standardized inputs make it round:

📄 In the paper

“It has been long known (LeCun et al., 1998b; Wiesler & Ney, 2011) that the network training converges faster if its inputs are whitened – i.e., linearly transformed to have zero means and unit variances, and decorrelated” Paper §2. And per-feature standardization alone already helps: “such normalization speeds up convergence, even when the features are not decorrelated” (§3, citing LeCun et al., 1998b).

Foundations

Correlation and whitening

Whitening goes one step further than standardizing: it also removes the correlations between features.

Two features are correlated when they tend to move together: tall people tend to be heavy. Standardizing each feature separately centres and rescales the cloud of points along each axis, but leaves it tilted. Whitening also un-tilts it, so the cloud becomes perfectly round: zero mean, unit variance and no correlation.

∑ The math (paper §2)
\[ \mathrm{Cov}[\mathrm{x}] = \mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}[\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}^T] - \mathrm{E}[\mathrm{x}]\,\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{x}]^T, \qquad \text{whitened: } \mathrm{Cov}[\mathrm{x}]^{-1/2}\,(\mathrm{x} - \mathrm{E}[\mathrm{x}]). \]

Here x is a whole vector of \(d\) features and \(\mathcal{X}\) is the set of all training examples. The covariance matrix \(\mathrm{Cov}[\mathrm{x}]\) is a \(d\times d\) table: the diagonal holds each feature’s variance, the off-diagonal entries how much two features vary together (the superscript \(T\) means “transpose”, turning a column into a row, so \(\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}^T\) is the table of all products \(x_j x_k\)). \(\mathrm{Cov}^{-1/2}\) is the matrix that “undoes” this table, like dividing by a standard deviation but in all directions at once. For \(d\) features that is \(d^2\) numbers to estimate and a cubic amount of work (about \(d^3\) steps) to invert, plus its derivatives for backpropagation.

📄 In the paper

“By whitening the inputs to each layer, we would take a step towards achieving the fixed distributions of inputs that would remove the ill effects of the internal covariate shift.” But “whitening the layer inputs is expensive, as it requires computing the covariance matrix … and its inverse square root … as well as the derivatives of these transforms for backpropagation” Paper §2. BN’s first simplification (Chapter 14) is to give up on decorrelation and standardize each feature on its own.

Foundations

Saturation and vanishing gradients

A sigmoid is flat at both ends: inputs that drift out there stop passing gradients back.

The sigmoid squashes any number into the range 0 to 1. Near 0 it is steep; far from 0 it is almost flat. The flat ends are called saturated: there, changing the input hardly changes the output, so the slope \(g'(x)\) (“g prime”) is almost zero. Backpropagation multiplies by this slope, so a saturated neuron passes almost no gradient back. Slide the distribution of inputs into the flat ends:

∑ The math (paper §1)
\[ g(x) = \frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)}, \qquad g'(x) = g(x)\,\big(1 - g(x)\big) \le \tfrac14. \]

\(\exp(-x)\) is \(e^{-x}\) with \(e \approx 2.718\). The slope is largest, \(\tfrac14\), at \(x = 0\), and “as \(|x|\) increases, \(g'(x)\) tends to zero”. Worked example: \(g(4) \approx 0.982\), so \(g'(4) \approx 0.982 \times 0.018 \approx 0.018\): fourteen times smaller than at 0.

In a deep network, the gradient reaching layer 1 has been multiplied by one such slope (and one weight matrix) per layer above it. Even at its best the sigmoid slope is \(\tfrac14\); if the inputs drift into the flat ends it is far smaller, and the product shrinks layer after layer: the vanishing gradient problem. “For all dimensions of \(x = Wu + b\) except those with small absolute values, the gradient flowing down to \(u\) will vanish and the model will train slowly. However, since \(x\) is affected by \(W\), \(b\) and the parameters of all the layers below, changes to those parameters during training will likely move many dimensions of \(x\) into the saturated regime of the nonlinearity and slow down the convergence. This effect is amplified as the network depth increases.” The usual remedies are ReLU (Nair & Hinton, 2010), careful initialization (Bengio & Glorot, 2010; Saxe et al., 2013) and small learning rates Paper §1.

Internal covariate shift

Covariate shift

A model trained on inputs from one distribution does worse when its inputs start coming from another.

Inputs to a model are sometimes called covariates. When the distribution of the inputs changes between training and use (a camera gets brighter, a sensor drifts), the model faces covariate shift: what it learned no longer fits what it sees. Below, a one-input classifier learns a threshold that separates two classes, then the inputs shift:

The usual fix for a whole model is domain adaptation: adjust the model, or the data, to the new distribution. Notice the toggle: when the shift is only a change of centre and scale, standardizing the input with its current mean and spread undoes it completely.

The paper’s key move is to look at a part of a network as a model of its own. Split a network into a lower part \(F_1\) and an upper part \(F_2\). From \(F_2\)’s point of view, the outputs of \(F_1\) are just its input data, and that data shifts every time \(F_1\)’s parameters change.

∑ The math (paper §1)
\[ \ell = F_2\big(F_1(u, \Theta_1), \Theta_2\big), \qquad \mathrm{x} = F_1(u, \Theta_1), \qquad \ell = F_2(\mathrm{x}, \Theta_2). \] \[ \Theta_2 \leftarrow \Theta_2 - \frac{\alpha}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{\partial F_2(\mathrm{x}_i, \Theta_2)}{\partial \Theta_2} \]

\(u\) is the network’s input, \(\Theta_1\) and \(\Theta_2\) the parameters of the lower and upper parts, x the signal passed between them. The gradient step for \(\Theta_2\) (with minibatch size \(m\) and learning rate \(\alpha\)) is “exactly equivalent to that for a stand-alone network \(F_2\) with input x”. So whatever helps a stand-alone network, such as inputs whose distribution stays the same, helps every sub-network too.

📄 In the paper

“When the input distribution to a learning system changes, it is said to experience covariate shift (Shimodaira, 2000). This is typically handled via domain adaptation (Jiang, 2008). However, the notion of covariate shift can be extended beyond the learning system as a whole, to apply to its parts, such as a sub-network or a layer … it is advantageous for the distribution of x to remain fixed over time. Then, \(\Theta_2\) does not have to readjust to compensate for the change in the distribution of x” Paper §1.

📄 In the paper

“We refer to the change in the distributions of internal nodes of a deep network, in the course of training, as Internal Covariate Shift. Eliminating it offers a promise of faster training” Paper §1. ⚠ Careful

Internal covariate shift is the paper’s explanation for why BN helps. The method works (Part V shows it); whether this is the right explanation was later debated (Chapter 34). Keep the two apart: the algorithm is exact, the story is a hypothesis.

Internal covariate shift

The big idea

Make normalization part of the network: standardize each pre-activation using the current minibatch, then let the network rescale it with two learned numbers.

If drifting distributions are the problem, fix the distributions. Before every nonlinearity, insert one step: take the pre-activation of each unit over the examples in the minibatch, subtract their mean and divide by their standard deviation (so it has mean 0 and spread 1 whatever the lower layers did), then multiply by a learned scale \(\gamma\) (gamma) and add a learned shift \(\beta\) (beta). That step is the Batch Normalizing Transform, or BN. Drift the pre-activations below and watch what reaches the sigmoid:

📄 In the paper

The abstract: “Our method draws its strength from making normalization a part of the model architecture and performing the normalization for each training mini-batch. Batch Normalization allows us to use much higher learning rates and be less careful about initialization, and in some cases eliminates the need for Dropout. Applied to a state-of-the-art image classification model, Batch Normalization achieves the same accuracy with 14 times fewer training steps, and beats the original model by a significant margin. Using an ensemble of batch-normalized networks, we improve upon the best published result on ImageNet classification: reaching 4.82% top-5 test error, exceeding the accuracy of human raters” Paper, Abstract. The introduction puts the speed-up as matching the original network “using only 7% of the training steps” (§1).

Internal covariate shift

Why normalizing outside the gradient fails

If the gradient step does not know that normalization will undo it, a parameter can grow forever while the loss never changes.

An obvious alternative to BN: train as usual, and every so often re-centre the activations using statistics measured over the training data, without telling the gradient about it. Section 2 of the paper shows why this goes wrong with the simplest possible case. A layer adds a learned bias \(b\) to its input \(u\), then subtracts the mean:

∑ The math (paper §2)
\[ \widehat{x} = x - \mathrm{E}[x], \quad x = u + b, \quad \mathrm{E}[x] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i. \]

If the gradient step ignores that \(\mathrm{E}[x]\) depends on \(b\), it updates \(b \leftarrow b + \Delta b\) with \(\Delta b \propto -\partial\ell/\partial\widehat{x}\). But then

\[ u + (b + \Delta b) - \mathrm{E}[u + (b + \Delta b)] = u + b - \mathrm{E}[u + b]. \]

The \(\Delta b\) appears once with a plus sign and once inside the mean with a minus sign, and cancels. The layer’s output, and so the loss, does not change, but \(b\) has moved. Next step, the same gradient, the same move: \(b\) grows without limit. Backpropagating through the mean instead gives \(\partial\ell/\partial b = \sum_i \partial\ell/\partial\widehat{x}_i \cdot (1 - 1) = 0\): the true gradient knows that \(b\) does nothing.

The general lesson: the normalization \(\widehat{\mathrm{x}} = \mathrm{Norm}(\mathrm{x}, \mathcal{X})\) depends not only on the example x but on all examples \(\mathcal{X}\). Backpropagation must include both dependencies. Doing that for full whitening is expensive:

📄 In the paper

“As the training continues, \(b\) will grow indefinitely while the loss remains fixed. This problem can get worse if the normalization not only centers but also scales the activations. We have observed this empirically in initial experiments, where the model blows up when the normalization parameters are computed outside the gradient descent step.” Hence the requirement: “for any parameter values, the network always produces activations with the desired distribution”, and backpropagation needs both Jacobians \(\partial\mathrm{Norm}(\mathrm{x},\mathcal{X})/\partial\mathrm{x}\) and \(\partial\mathrm{Norm}(\mathrm{x},\mathcal{X})/\partial\mathcal{X}\); “ignoring the latter term would lead to the explosion described above” Paper §2. The paper also rejects statistics computed from a single example (Lyu & Simoncelli, 2008), because they discard “the absolute scale of activations”.

The Batch Normalizing Transform

Two simplifications

Normalize each feature on its own, and estimate its statistics from the minibatch.

Full whitening of every layer is too expensive (Chapter 13). The paper makes “two necessary simplifications”.

First: one feature at a time. Instead of whitening a layer’s \(d\) activations jointly, standardize each one separately: mean 0, variance 1, and forget about correlations. Each column of the table below is one activation \(x^{(k)}\), each row one example; each column is normalized using only its own numbers:

∑ The math (paper §3)
\[ \widehat{x}^{(k)} = \frac{x^{(k)} - \mathrm{E}[x^{(k)}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x^{(k)}]}} \qquad\text{for each dimension } k = 1, \dots, d \text{ of } \mathrm{x} = (x^{(1)} \dots x^{(d)}). \]

The superscript \((k)\) is an index, not a power: \(x^{(k)}\) is the \(k\)-th activation of the layer. Here “the expectation and variance are computed over the training data set”; the second simplification replaces that with the minibatch.

Second: minibatch statistics. Computing \(\mathrm{E}\) and \(\mathrm{Var}\) over the whole training set after every step is impractical with SGD. But each minibatch already gives estimates of them, and because they are computed inside the network, “the statistics used for normalization can fully participate in the gradient backpropagation”.

The two simplifications fit together. A minibatch of \(m\) examples can estimate one variance per feature easily, but it cannot estimate a \(d\times d\) covariance when \(m\) is smaller than \(d\): the estimated covariance matrix is singular (it has zero eigenvalues, directions with no spread at all), and cannot be inverted:

📄 In the paper

“Note that the use of mini-batches is enabled by computation of per-dimension variances rather than joint covariances; in the joint case, regularization would be required since the mini-batch size is likely to be smaller than the number of activations being whitened, resulting in singular covariance matrices” Paper §3.

The Batch Normalizing Transform

Algorithm 1, step by step

Four lines: minibatch mean, minibatch variance, normalize, scale and shift.

Focus on one activation \(x\) (drop the \(k\)) and a minibatch \(\mathcal{B} = \{x_1, \dots, x_m\}\) of its \(m\) values. The Batch Normalizing Transform \(\mathrm{BN}_{\gamma,\beta}\) turns these into \(y_1, \dots, y_m\):

Input: values of \(x\) over a mini-batch: \(\mathcal{B} = \{x_{1\dots m}\}\); parameters to be learned: \(\gamma, \beta\)
Output: \(\{y_i = \mathrm{BN}_{\gamma,\beta}(x_i)\}\)
1 · \(\mu_\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} x_i\)   // mini-batch mean
2 · \(\sigma_\mathcal{B}^2 \leftarrow \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} (x_i - \mu_\mathcal{B})^2\)   // mini-batch variance
3 · \(\widehat{x}_i \leftarrow \dfrac{x_i - \mu_\mathcal{B}}{\sqrt{\sigma_\mathcal{B}^2 + \epsilon}}\)   // normalize
4 · \(y_i \leftarrow \gamma\,\widehat{x}_i + \beta \equiv \mathrm{BN}_{\gamma,\beta}(x_i)\)   // scale and shift

Algorithm 1 of the paper (line numbers added). The paper calls it “Batch Normalizing Transform, applied to activation x over a mini-batch”.

∑ The symbols

\(m\): the minibatch size. \(\mu_\mathcal{B}\): mean of the \(m\) values (subscript \(\mathcal{B}\): “of this batch”). \(\sigma^2_\mathcal{B}\): their variance, dividing by \(m\) (not \(m-1\); see Chapter 20). \(\widehat{x}_i\): the normalized value. \(\epsilon\) (epsilon): “a constant added to the mini-batch variance for numerical stability”. \(\gamma, \beta\): the learned scale and shift. \(\leftarrow\): “is set to”. \(\equiv\): “is the same thing as”. The Batch Normalizing Transform

γ and β: keeping what the layer could do

Two learned numbers per activation let BN represent the identity, so normalizing never costs the network anything it could do before.

Normalizing alone would change what the network can compute. Take a sigmoid: normalized inputs mostly lie between −2 and 2, where the sigmoid is nearly a straight line. The bend, which is what makes a network more than a linear model, would hardly be used. So BN adds a learned scale \(\gamma\) and shift \(\beta\) per activation. With the right values they undo the normalization exactly:

∑ The math (paper §3)
\[ y^{(k)} = \gamma^{(k)}\,\widehat{x}^{(k)} + \beta^{(k)}, \qquad \gamma^{(k)} = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x^{(k)}]},\ \ \beta^{(k)} = \mathrm{E}[x^{(k)}] \ \Rightarrow\ y^{(k)} = x^{(k)}. \]

Check: \(\gamma\widehat{x} + \beta = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}}\cdot\frac{x - \mathrm{E}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}}} + \mathrm{E} = x\). These parameters “are learned along with the original model parameters, and restore the representation power of the network”. The difference: the network now chooses the mean and spread of each activation directly through \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\), instead of through a tangle of all the weights below.

📄 In the paper

“Note that simply normalizing each input of a layer may change what the layer can represent. For instance, normalizing the inputs of a sigmoid would constrain them to the linear regime of the nonlinearity. To address this, we make sure that the transformation inserted in the network can represent the identity transform” Paper §3. The conclusion counts the cost: “Batch Normalization adds only two extra parameters per activation, and in doing so preserves the representation ability of the network” (§5).

The Batch Normalizing Transform

One example, many batch-mates

An example’s BN output depends on which other examples share its minibatch.

Every layer you have met so far processes each example on its own. BN does not: \(\mu_\mathcal{B}\) and \(\sigma^2_\mathcal{B}\) are computed from the whole batch, so the same input value gives a slightly different output depending on its batch-mates. Inside each batch, though, the normalized values always satisfy two exact identities, whatever the batch:

∑ The math (paper §3)
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{m} \widehat{x}_i = 0, \qquad \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \widehat{x}_i^{\,2} = 1 \quad (\text{neglecting } \epsilon). \]

The first because the deviations from a mean always sum to 0; the second because \(\frac1m\sum(x_i-\mu)^2 = \sigma^2\) and we divided each deviation by \(\sigma\). Taking expectations, “the distributions of values of any \(\widehat{x}\) has the expected value of 0 and the variance of 1, as long as the elements of each mini-batch are sampled from the same distribution”.

📄 In the paper

“It should be noted that the BN transform does not independently process the activation in each training example. Rather, \(\mathrm{BN}_{\gamma,\beta}(x)\) depends both on the training example and the other examples in the mini-batch” Paper §3. Two consequences come later: at test time this dependence must be removed (Chapter 20), and during training it acts like noise that regularizes (Chapter 27). The normalized \(\widehat{x}^{(k)}\) can be seen as inputs to a sub-network \(y^{(k)} = \gamma^{(k)}\widehat{x}^{(k)} + \beta^{(k)}\) followed by the rest of the network, and “these sub-network inputs all have fixed means and variances”.

The Batch Normalizing Transform

Backpropagation through BN

Six chain-rule equations carry the gradient through all four lines of Algorithm 1, including through the batch mean and variance.

For BN to be part of the network, gradients must flow through it: to the learned \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\), and back to every input \(x_i\). Because \(\mu_\mathcal{B}\) and \(\sigma^2_\mathcal{B}\) are computed from all the \(x_i\), each \(x_i\) influences the loss three ways: directly through its own \(\widehat{x}_i\), through the mean, and through the variance. This is exactly the dependence Chapter 13 showed must not be ignored.

∑ The math (paper §3)
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \widehat{x}_i} &= \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial y_i}\cdot\gamma \qquad\text{(B1)}\\ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma_\mathcal{B}^2} &= \sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \widehat{x}_i}\cdot(x_i - \mu_\mathcal{B})\cdot\frac{-1}{2}\,(\sigma_\mathcal{B}^2+\epsilon)^{-3/2} \qquad\text{(B2)}\\ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu_\mathcal{B}} &= \sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \widehat{x}_i}\cdot\frac{-1}{\sqrt{\sigma_\mathcal{B}^2+\epsilon}} \qquad\text{(B3)}\\ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial x_i} &= \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \widehat{x}_i}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_\mathcal{B}^2+\epsilon}} + \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma_\mathcal{B}^2}\cdot\frac{2(x_i-\mu_\mathcal{B})}{m} + \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu_\mathcal{B}}\cdot\frac{1}{m} \qquad\text{(B4)}\\ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \gamma} &= \sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial y_i}\cdot\widehat{x}_i \qquad\text{(B5)}\\ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \beta} &= \sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial y_i} \qquad\text{(B6)} \end{aligned} \]

The labels B1–B6 are this book’s; the paper prints the six lines unnumbered. Read them with the two rules of Chapter 3. B1: line 4 multiplies \(\widehat{x}_i\) by \(\gamma\). B2: line 3 divides by \((\sigma^2+\epsilon)^{1/2}\); the slope of \(s^{-1/2}\) is \(-\tfrac12 s^{-3/2}\), and \(\sigma^2\) is used by all \(m\) examples, hence the sum. B3: line 3 subtracts \(\mu\), slope \(-1/\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}\), summed over all examples. (\(\mu\) also enters line 2; that path adds \(\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\sigma^2}\cdot\frac1m\sum -2(x_i - \mu)\), which is exactly 0 because deviations sum to 0, so the paper leaves it out.) B4: \(x_i\) reaches the loss three ways: through \(\widehat{x}_i\) directly, through \(\sigma^2\) (line 2, slope \(2(x_i-\mu)/m\)) and through \(\mu\) (line 1, slope \(1/m\)); the three contributions add. B5, B6: \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) are shared by all examples, so their gradients are sums.

Now trace them with numbers. Pick a batch and some upstream gradients \(\partial\ell/\partial y_i\) (what the layers above send back). The tracer evaluates B1–B6 in order and checks the result two independent ways: by nudging each \(x_i\) and re-running the forward pass (finite differences), and against the shared library’s automatic backpropagation:

The Batch Normalizing Transform

What the BN gradient removes

The gradient through BN is the upstream gradient with two parts taken out: the part that would shift the batch, and the part that would stretch it.

Substitute B1–B3 into B4 and simplify. Writing \(g_i = \partial\ell/\partial y_i\) and \(\bar{g}\) for its batch average, everything collapses into one line, the form PyTorch actually computes:

∑ The math (derived from B1–B4)
\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial x_i} = \frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma_\mathcal{B}^2+\epsilon}}\Big( g_i - \bar{g} - \widehat{x}_i \cdot \overline{g\,\widehat{x}} \Big), \qquad \bar{g} = \frac1m\sum_j g_j,\quad \overline{g\widehat{x}} = \frac1m\sum_j g_j\widehat{x}_j. \]

In words: start from the upstream gradient \(g\); subtract its average (the part that would move every \(x_i\) together, a shift that BN would cancel anyway); subtract its projection onto \(\widehat{x}\) (the part that would stretch the batch, which BN would also cancel); then scale by \(\gamma/\sigma\). Consequences that hold exactly (with \(\epsilon = 0\)): \(\sum_i \partial\ell/\partial x_i = 0\) and \(\sum_i \widehat{x}_i\,\partial\ell/\partial x_i = 0\).

⌨ In the code

PyTorch’s CPU backward pass (Normalization.cpp L419–476) computes dotp = Σ (x − mean)·go, then k = dotp · invstd² / n, grad_mean = Σ go / n, and dx = (go − grad_mean − (x − mean)·k) · invstd · w: the formula above, with w = γ. Its comment reads “dL/dX = (Q(dL/dY) − dot(Y, dL/dY) * Y) / sigma * w”. The gradients of γ and β are dotp · invstd (B5) and the sum of go (B6). The shared NN.batchNorm uses the same form.

Batch-normalized networks

Training mode and inference mode

At test time, BN swaps the minibatch statistics for fixed population statistics, so each prediction depends only on its own input.

During training, BN’s dependence on batch-mates is useful. At test time it is not: a photo’s label should not depend on which other photos happen to be classified with it, and sometimes there is only one photo. So a trained network switches BN into inference mode: it normalizes with the population statistics \(\mathrm{E}[x]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}[x]\) of the training data, which are fixed numbers. Here is a trained toy BN network classifying one test glyph:

∑ The math (paper §3.1)
\[ \widehat{x} = \frac{x - \mathrm{E}[x]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x] + \epsilon}}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}[x] = \frac{m}{m-1}\cdot\mathrm{E}_\mathcal{B}[\sigma_\mathcal{B}^2]. \]

\(\mathrm{E}[x]\) is the average of the minibatch means \(\mu_\mathcal{B}\) over many training minibatches, and \(\mathrm{E}_\mathcal{B}[\sigma^2_\mathcal{B}]\) the average of their variances. The factor \(\frac{m}{m-1}\) is there because a variance computed around the batch’s own mean (dividing by \(m\)) comes out too small on average, by exactly the factor \(\frac{m-1}{m}\): the batch mean sits closer to the batch’s own points than the true mean does. Multiplying by \(\frac{m}{m-1}\) gives an unbiased estimate. Worked example: \(m = 4\): if the true variance is 1, batch variances average \(3/4 = 0.75\), and \(\frac{4}{3}\times 0.75 = 1\).

📄 In the paper

“The normalization of activations that depends on the mini-batch allows efficient training, but is neither necessary nor desirable during inference; we want the output to depend only on the input, deterministically. For this, once the network has been trained, we use the normalization … using the population, rather than mini-batch, statistics. Neglecting \(\epsilon\), these normalized activations have the same mean 0 and variance 1 as during training. We use the unbiased variance estimate … Using moving averages instead, we can track the accuracy of a model as it trains” Paper §3.1.

⌨ In the code

PyTorch keeps the moving averages as buffers running_mean (starts at 0) and running_var (starts at 1) (batchnorm.py L61–66). In training mode, each step does running_mean = momentum·mean + (1 − momentum)·running_mean and stores the unbiased variance var_sum / (n − 1) (Normalization.cpp L235–239), with momentum = 0.1 by default (L42); model.eval() switches to the buffers (L183–186). The docstring warns that this “momentum” is not an optimizer’s momentum: it is the weight of the new value (L298–303). The toy networks in this book do the same.

Batch-normalized networks

Algorithm 2 and folding BN away

Train with batch statistics, then freeze the population statistics, and BN becomes one fixed multiply-and-add.

Algorithm 2 is the full recipe: build the training network, train it, then build the inference network.

Input: network \(N\) with trainable parameters \(\Theta\); subset of activations \(\{x^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}\)
Output: batch-normalized network for inference, \(N^{\mathrm{inf}}_{\mathrm{BN}}\)
1 · \(N^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{BN}} \leftarrow N\)   // training BN network
2 · for \(k = 1 \dots K\) do
3 ·    add transformation \(y^{(k)} = \mathrm{BN}_{\gamma^{(k)},\beta^{(k)}}(x^{(k)})\) to \(N^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{BN}}\) (Alg. 1)
4 ·    modify each layer in \(N^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{BN}}\) with input \(x^{(k)}\) to take \(y^{(k)}\) instead
5 · end for
6 · train \(N^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{BN}}\) to optimize the parameters \(\Theta \cup \{\gamma^{(k)}, \beta^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}\)
7 · \(N^{\mathrm{inf}}_{\mathrm{BN}} \leftarrow N^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{BN}}\)   // inference BN network with frozen parameters
8 · for \(k = 1 \dots K\) do
9 ·    // for clarity, \(x \equiv x^{(k)}, \gamma \equiv \gamma^{(k)}, \mu_\mathcal{B} \equiv \mu_\mathcal{B}^{(k)}\), etc.
10 ·   process multiple training mini-batches \(\mathcal{B}\), each of size \(m\), and average over them: \(\mathrm{E}[x] \leftarrow \mathrm{E}_\mathcal{B}[\mu_\mathcal{B}]\), \(\mathrm{Var}[x] \leftarrow \frac{m}{m-1}\mathrm{E}_\mathcal{B}[\sigma^2_\mathcal{B}]\)
11 ·   in \(N^{\mathrm{inf}}_{\mathrm{BN}}\), replace the transform \(y = \mathrm{BN}_{\gamma,\beta}(x)\) with \(y = \frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x]+\epsilon}}\cdot x + \Big(\beta - \frac{\gamma\,\mathrm{E}[x]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x]+\epsilon}}\Big)\)
12 · end for

Line 11 is worth a second look. With fixed statistics, BN is just \(y = a\,x + c\): a multiplication and an addition. And \(x = Wu\) is itself linear, so the BN layer can be folded into the weights: \(y = (aW)u + c\). At inference time a batch-normalized network costs nothing extra:

∑ The math (line 11)
\[ y = \gamma\,\frac{x - \mathrm{E}[x]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x]+\epsilon}} + \beta = \underbrace{\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x]+\epsilon}}}_{a}\, x + \underbrace{\Big(\beta - \frac{\gamma\,\mathrm{E}[x]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x]+\epsilon}}\Big)}_{c}. \]

Expand the first form and group the terms with and without \(x\): that is all line 11 does. Worked example: \(\gamma = 2\), \(\beta = 1\), \(\mathrm{E}[x] = 3\), \(\mathrm{Var}[x] = 4\), \(\epsilon = 0\): \(a = 2/2 = 1\), \(c = 1 - 2\cdot3/2 = -2\), so \(y = x - 2\). Check at \(x = 5\): \(2\cdot(5-3)/2 + 1 = 3 = 5 - 2\). ✓

📄 In the paper

“Since the means and variances are fixed during inference, the normalization is simply a linear transform applied to each activation. It may further be composed with the scaling by \(\gamma\) and shift by \(\beta\), to yield a single linear transform that replaces \(\mathrm{BN}(x)\)” Paper §3.1, Algorithm 2. The conclusion lists “deterministic inference that does not depend on the mini-batch” among BN’s differences from earlier work (§5).

The smaller the minibatch, the noisier its mean and variance, and with one example there is nothing to normalize with.

The paper requires “a mini-batch size \(m > 1\)” for training: with \(m = 1\), \(\mu_\mathcal{B} = x_1\), every \(\widehat{x}\) is \(0\), and the network sees nothing. Batch-normalized networks

Where BN goes: before the nonlinearity

Normalize \(Wu\), the input to the nonlinearity, not \(u\); and drop the bias, because BN’s mean subtraction cancels it.

A layer \(z = g(Wu + b)\) has two candidate places for BN: its input \(u\), or the pre-activation \(Wu + b\). The paper picks the second. Its reason: \(u\) is the output of the previous nonlinearity (say a ReLU), so its shape is lopsided, with a spike at zero and a long tail, and that shape itself changes during training; fixing only its mean and variance would not stabilize it. \(Wu + b\) is a weighted sum of many inputs, which tends to look symmetric and bell-shaped, “more Gaussian”. Once BN is applied to \(Wu + b\), the bias \(b\) is useless: adding the same \(b\) to every example raises the batch mean by \(b\), and the subtraction removes it again (the same cancellation as in Chapter 13, now harmless because BN’s gradient knows about it). The shift \(\beta\) takes over the bias’s job:

∑ The math (paper §3.2)
\[ z = g(Wu + b) \quad\longrightarrow\quad z = g(\mathrm{BN}(Wu)) \]

“where the BN transform is applied independently to each dimension of \(\mathrm{x} = Wu\), with a separate pair of learned parameters \(\gamma^{(k)}, \beta^{(k)}\) per dimension.” Because \(\mathrm{BN}(Wu + b) = \mathrm{BN}(Wu)\) for any constant \(b\), dropping \(b\) changes nothing.

📄 In the paper

“We add the BN transform immediately before the nonlinearity, by normalizing \(\mathrm{x} = Wu + b\). We could have also normalized the layer inputs \(u\), but since \(u\) is likely the output of another nonlinearity, the shape of its distribution is likely to change during training, and constraining its first and second moments would not eliminate the covariate shift. In contrast, \(Wu + b\) is more likely to have a symmetric, non-sparse distribution, that is ‘more Gaussian’ (Hyvärinen & Oja, 2000) … the bias \(b\) can be ignored since its effect will be canceled by the subsequent mean subtraction (the role of the bias is subsumed by \(\beta\) in Alg. 1)” Paper §3.2. The conclusion contrasts this with the standardization layer of Gülçehre & Bengio (2013), applied to the output of the nonlinearity (§5).

⌨ In the code

This is why networks built with PyTorch usually write nn.Conv2d(..., bias=False) followed by nn.BatchNorm2d: the BN module’s bias parameter is \(\beta\) (batchnorm.py L93–97). The toy networks in this book build their BN layers without a bias (bn.js, T.Net).

Batch-normalized networks

BN for convolutional layers

In an image network, one feature map shares one mean, one variance, one γ and one β, pooled over the batch and every location.

A convolutional layer slides a small learned filter across an image and writes the result at every location, producing a feature map: a \(p\times q\) grid of numbers, one per location. A layer has several filters and so several feature maps (also called channels). Because the same filter is used everywhere, the same feature should be treated the same way at every location: the convolutional property. So BN treats all \(p\cdot q\) locations of a feature map, in all \(m\) images of the batch, as one big batch of values.

∑ The math (paper §3.2)
\[ m' = |\mathcal{B}| = m\cdot p\,q \]

In Algorithm 1, \(\mathcal{B}\) becomes “the set of all values in a feature map across both the elements of a mini-batch and spatial locations”. \(|\mathcal{B}|\) is the number of values in it, the effective mini-batch \(m'\). Worked example: a batch of \(m = 32\) images with \(14\times14\) feature maps gives \(m' = 32\cdot196 = 6{,}272\) values per statistic. One \(\gamma\) and one \(\beta\) per feature map, not per location.

📄 In the paper

“For convolutional layers, we additionally want the normalization to obey the convolutional property – so that different elements of the same feature map, at different locations, are normalized in the same way. To achieve this, we jointly normalize all the activations in a mini-batch, over all locations … We learn a pair of parameters \(\gamma^{(k)}\) and \(\beta^{(k)}\) per feature map, rather than per activation. Alg. 2 is modified similarly, so that during inference the BN transform applies the same linear transformation to each activation in a given feature map” Paper §3.2.

⌨ In the code

nn.BatchNorm2d takes inputs of shape (N, C, H, W) and keeps one statistic per channel C (batchnorm.py L378). The kernel counts the values per channel as n = input.numel() / n_input (Normalization.cpp L206–208): all elements divided by the number of channels, i.e. \(m\cdot p q\).

Batch-normalized networks

Scale invariance and higher learning rates

Multiply a layer’s weights by any factor and BN’s output does not change; its gradient with respect to those weights shrinks by the same factor.

Chapter 4 described the vicious circle of large learning rates: big steps grow the weights, bigger weights amplify the gradients, bigger gradients make even bigger steps, and the model explodes. BN breaks the circle. Scaling \(W\) by \(a\) scales every \(x_i = Wu_i\) by \(a\), which scales \(\mu_\mathcal{B}\) and \(\sigma_\mathcal{B}\) by \(a\) too, and the ratio does not change. Check it on a real layer:

∑ The math (paper §3.3)
\[ \mathrm{BN}(Wu) = \mathrm{BN}((aW)u), \qquad \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{BN}((aW)u)}{\partial u} = \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{BN}(Wu)}{\partial u}, \qquad \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{BN}((aW)u)}{\partial (aW)} = \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{BN}(Wu)}{\partial W}. \]

\(a\) is any positive number (ignoring \(\epsilon\)). The first: \(\frac{a x - a\mu}{a\sigma} = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\). The second: since the output does not depend on \(a\), neither does its slope with respect to the input \(u\): the layer’s Jacobian (the table of all slopes \(\partial\,\text{output}_j/\partial\,\text{input}_i\)) is unchanged, so gradient flowing back to lower layers is unchanged. The third: the output is the same function of \(aW\) as of \(W\), but stretched by \(a\) along the weight axis, so its slope is \(a\) times flatter. “So larger weights lead to smaller gradients.”

That last property makes training self-stabilizing. If a large learning rate makes \(W\) grow, the gradient with respect to \(W\) shrinks in proportion, and the effective step (relative to the size of \(W\)) shrinks with the square of the growth. “Batch Normalization also makes training more resilient to the parameter scale. Normally, large learning rates may increase the scale of layer parameters, which then amplify the gradient during backpropagation and lead to the model explosion. However, with Batch Normalization, backpropagation through a layer is unaffected by the scale of its parameters … so the scale does not affect the layer Jacobian nor, consequently, the gradient propagation … larger weights lead to smaller gradients, and Batch Normalization will stabilize the parameter growth” Paper §3.3. Batch-normalized networks

Jacobians with singular values near 1

A conjecture: between normalized layers, the network’s map tends to preserve lengths, so gradients neither explode nor vanish.

A layer’s Jacobian \(J\) is a matrix that turns small changes of its input into changes of its output; backpropagation multiplies gradients by \(J^T\) once per layer. How \(J\) stretches vectors is described by its singular values: draw all inputs of length 1 (a circle), apply \(J\), and you get an ellipse whose half-axes are the singular values. If they are all 1, \(J\) is orthogonal: a pure rotation that preserves lengths. Through many layers, singular values above 1 multiply into explosions and below 1 into vanishing gradients.

∑ The math (paper §3.3)
\[ \widehat{z} = F(\widehat{x}) \approx J\widehat{x}, \qquad I = \mathrm{Cov}[\widehat{z}] = J\,\mathrm{Cov}[\widehat{x}]\,J^T = J J^T. \]

\(\widehat{x}\) and \(\widehat{z}\) are two consecutive normalized vectors, and \(F\) the transformation between them, approximated by the linear map \(J\). If \(\widehat{x}\) and \(\widehat{z}\) are Gaussian and uncorrelated with unit variances, their covariances are the identity \(I\); a linear map transforms covariance as \(J\,\mathrm{Cov}\,J^T\); so \(JJ^T = I\), which is the definition of an orthogonal matrix. BN only guarantees unit variances (the diagonal), not zero correlations, so in reality \(JJ^T\) has 1s on its diagonal but not necessarily 0s elsewhere. The widget shows both cases: per-feature normalization pulls the singular values towards 1 (their squares average exactly 1), full decorrelation makes them exactly 1.

📄 In the paper

“We further conjecture that Batch Normalization may lead the layer Jacobians to have singular values close to 1, which is known to be beneficial for training (Saxe et al., 2013) … Although the above assumptions are not true in reality, we expect Batch Normalization to help make gradient propagation better behaved. This remains an area of further study” Paper §3.3. The paper labels this a conjecture; so does this book.

Batch-normalized networks

BN as a regularizer

Random batch-mates jiggle every example’s activations a little each time it is seen, a noise that discourages memorizing.

Overfitting is when a network memorizes its training examples instead of learning patterns that carry over to new ones. Techniques that fight it are called regularizers. Dropout is one: it switches off random units, so the network sees a slightly different version of each example every time. BN produces a similar effect for free: because \(\mu_\mathcal{B}\) and \(\sigma_\mathcal{B}\) depend on random batch-mates (Chapter 17), a training example’s activations are slightly different each time it appears, but only if its batch-mates change. The experiments

ImageNet and the Inception network

The real test: a large convolutional network on 1,000 classes of photographs, where training normally takes tens of millions of steps.

ImageNet (the ILSVRC 2012 classification task) has about 1.2 million training photos in 1,000 classes, and a held-out set of 50,000 validation photos. Two standard scores: top-1 accuracy (the network’s single best guess is right) and top-5 error (the right class is not among its five best guesses). The paper evaluates its training curves by single-crop top-1 accuracy on the validation set, which it calls “validation accuracy @1”.

The network is a new variant of Inception (GoogLeNet; Szegedy et al., 2014), a deep convolutional network built from blocks that run several filter sizes side by side. The paper’s variant replaces every 5×5 convolution by two consecutive 3×3 convolutions (with up to 128 filters): the same reach, fewer weights.

📄 In the paper

The network “has a large number of convolutional and pooling layers, with a softmax layer to predict the image class, out of 1000 possibilities. Convolutional layers use ReLU as the nonlinearity … the 5×5 convolutional layers are replaced by two consecutive layers of 3×3 convolutions with up to 128 filters. The network contains \(13.6\cdot10^6\) parameters, and, other than the top softmax layer, has no fully-connected layers.” Training ran on a distributed system (Dean et al., 2012), “using 5 concurrent steps on each of 10 model replicas, using asynchronous SGD with momentum (Sutskever et al., 2013), with the mini-batch size of 32.” In every BN variant, “Batch Normalization was applied to the input of each nonlinearity, in a convolutional way, as described in section 3.2, while keeping the rest of the architecture constant” Paper §4.2.

Limits and where this leads

BN made deep networks easy to train, but it ties every example to its minibatch, and the reason it works turned out to be debated.

What the paper leaves open. The conclusion lists its own future work: applying BN to recurrent neural networks (networks that process sequences step by step, where “the internal covariate shift and the vanishing or exploding gradients may be especially severe”); more study of the regularization effect; whether recomputing the population statistics on new data could help domain adaptation (Chapter 10); and more theory. It also says BN “bears similarity to the standardization layer of (Gülçehre & Bengio, 2013)”, which normalizes the outputs of nonlinearities instead of their inputs.

The batch is a weakness. BN needs a reasonable number of examples per minibatch, and it behaves differently in training and inference. When the minibatch is small (big images or big models on limited memory), the statistics get noisy, and the training-time network and the inference-time network drift apart:

What came next. Later methods normalize over something other than the batch, so each example is handled on its own. Layer Normalization (Ba, Kiros & Hinton, 2016) normalizes over the units of a layer within one example, and became the standard in Transformers (lesson 8) and the language models built on them. Group Normalization (Wu & He, 2018) does something similar for images. In vision, BN stayed central: ResNet (lesson 5) puts it after every convolution, and it is what lets that network train with 152 layers.

Was internal covariate shift the reason? Santurkar, Tsipras, Ilyas & Madry (“How Does Batch Normalization Help Optimization?”, NeurIPS 2018) found that BN still helps when noise is deliberately injected after it to create covariate shift, and argued that its main effect is to make the loss surface smoother, so gradients are more reliable and larger steps safe. That fits the paper’s §3.3 better than its §2: the scale-invariance and Jacobian arguments (Ch. 25, 26) are about better-behaved gradients. The algorithm in this book is unaffected; only the explanation is contested.

📄 In the paper

“In this work, we have not explored the full range of possibilities that Batch Normalization potentially enables. Our future work includes applications of our method to Recurrent Neural Networks … More study is needed of the regularization properties of Batch Normalization … We plan to investigate whether Batch Normalization can help with domain adaptation … perhaps with just a recomputation of the population means and variances (Alg. 2). Finally, we believe that further theoretical analysis of the algorithm would allow still more improvements and applications” Paper §5.

Glossary

Activation (unit)
One number computed inside a network for one example, such as a neuron’s pre-activation or output.
Batch Normalization (BN)
Standardizing each activation with the mean and variance of the current minibatch, then scaling by a learned γ and shifting by a learned β (Algorithm 1).
Batch Normalizing Transform
The paper’s name for BNγ,β: x1…m → y1…m.
Batch-mates
The other examples in the same minibatch, which BN’s statistics depend on.
Chain rule
The rule that the slope of a chain of steps is the product of the steps’ slopes; gradients from several uses of a value add up.
Channel
One feature map of a convolutional layer.
Convolutional layer
A layer that slides small learned filters over an image, producing one feature map per filter.
Convolutional property
Treating the same feature the same way at every location; BN keeps it by sharing statistics across locations.
Correlation
How much two features tend to move together.
Covariance matrix
The d×d table of variances (diagonal) and covariances (off-diagonal) of d features.
Covariate shift
A change in the distribution of a model’s inputs between training and use.
Covariates
Another word for a model’s input features.
Curvature
How sharply a loss bends; steep curvature needs small learning rates.
Deep network
A neural network with many hidden layers.
Distribution
The pattern of values a quantity takes across many examples.
Divergence
Training blowing up: the parameters or loss grow without limit.
Domain adaptation
Adjusting a model or its data to a new input distribution.
Dropout
A regularizer that switches off random units during training (Srivastava et al., 2014).
Effective mini-batch
m′ = m·pq, the number of values per statistic in convolutional BN.
Ensemble
Several separately trained models whose predictions are combined, here by averaging class probabilities.
Epsilon (ε)
A small constant added to the variance in BN for numerical stability (PyTorch: 10⁻⁵).
Expected value
E[x], the long-run average of a random quantity.
Exponential learning-rate decay
Multiplying the learning rate by a fixed factor at regular intervals.
Feature map
The grid of outputs of one convolutional filter, one value per location.
Folding
Merging an inference-mode BN (a fixed multiply and add) into the preceding layer’s weights and bias.
Gaussian (normal) distribution
The bell-shaped distribution; about 70% of it lies within 1.04 standard deviations of the mean.
Histogram
A chart of how many values fall into each bin of the number line.
ImageNet
The ILSVRC image-classification benchmark: about 1.2 million training photos in 1,000 classes, 50,000 validation photos.
Inception
The deep convolutional network family (GoogLeNet) the paper builds on; its variant has 13.6 million parameters.
Inference mode
Using BN with fixed population statistics, so each prediction depends only on its own input.
Internal covariate shift
The change in the distributions of a network’s internal activations caused by changing parameters during training.
Jacobian
The table of slopes of each output of a layer with respect to each input.
Layer
A group of neurons that all read the same inputs; networks stack them.
Layer Normalization
A later method that normalizes over the units of one example instead of over the batch (Ba, Kiros & Hinton, 2016).
L2 weight regularization
Adding λ·Σw² to the loss to keep weights small (weight decay).
Local Response Normalization
An older image-network layer dividing each activation by its neighbouring channels’ activity; BN made it unnecessary.
Mean
The average of some values, μ.
Median
The middle value, the 50th percentile.
Minibatch
The small random group of m examples used for one SGD step, and the group BN computes its statistics over.
MNIST
A data set of 70,000 handwritten digits of 28×28 pixels.
Moving average
A running estimate updated each step as new = (1 − momentum)·old + momentum·value; PyTorch uses momentum 0.1 for BN’s statistics.
Multi-crop evaluation
Classifying many crops of one image and averaging the predictions.
Nonlinearity
Another name for the activation function.
Orthogonal matrix
A matrix J with JJᵀ = I: a rotation that preserves lengths.
Percentile
The value below which a given percentage of the values lie.
Photometric distortions
Random changes of brightness, contrast and colour applied to training images.
Population statistics
E[x] and Var[x] of an activation over the training data, used by BN at inference.
Pre-activation
The weighted sum x = Wu + b before the nonlinearity; where BN is applied.
Regularizer
Anything that discourages overfitting.
Saturation
The flat ends of a sigmoid, where its slope is nearly zero and gradients vanish.
Scale invariance
BN((aW)u) = BN(Wu): BN’s output ignores the scale of the weights before it.
Singular matrix
A matrix with a zero eigenvalue, which cannot be inverted.
Singular values
The half-axes of the ellipse into which a matrix maps the unit circle: how much it stretches in each direction.
Standard deviation
σ, the square root of the variance.
Standardizing
Subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, giving mean 0 and variance 1 (a z-score).
Top-1 / top-5 error
The fraction of images whose correct class is not the model’s first guess / not among its five best guesses.
Training mode
Using BN with the statistics of the current minibatch.
Unbiased estimate
An estimate that is right on average; for variance, multiply the batch variance by m/(m − 1).
Vanishing gradient
Gradients that shrink layer after layer until the lower layers barely learn.
Variance
σ², the average squared distance from the mean.
Whitening
Transforming features to zero mean, unit variance and no correlation.
