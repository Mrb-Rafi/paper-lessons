Dropout,
from zero.

In 2014 five researchers in Toronto published a strangely simple trick: while a neural network learns, switch off a random half of its units for every example it sees. The network cannot rely on any one unit, stops memorising its training data, and does better on new data. This book starts from “what is a neuron?” and ends with every equation, figure, table and result of the paper, including why the trick is a cheap way of averaging an astronomical number of networks. You need no background: every idea comes with something to play with.

Based on Nitish Srivastava, Geoffrey Hinton, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, Ruslan Salakhutdinov, “Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting”, Journal of Machine Learning Research 15 (2014) and the first author’s code at nitishsrivastava/deepnet. Foundations

Neurons, layers and the forward pass

A neural network is a stack of layers; each unit in a layer adds up weighted inputs and bends the sum with a simple function.

A computer that recognises a handwritten “4” has to turn a grid of pixel brightnesses into an answer. A neural network does this with many tiny, identical calculators called units or neurons. One unit takes a few numbers in, multiplies each by its own weight, adds them up with a bias, and passes the total through an activation function:

Two activation functions appear in the paper. The logistic (or sigmoid) function \(f(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})\) squashes any number into the range 0 to 1. The rectified linear unit (ReLU), \(f(x) = \max(0, x)\), passes positive numbers and replaces negative ones by 0. Without an activation function, stacking units would only ever compute straight-line (linear) functions.

Units are organised in layers. The input layer just holds the data (for a 28×28 image, 784 numbers). Each hidden layer reads the outputs of the layer below. The output layer gives the answer, for digits one score per class. Computing layer after layer, from input to output, is the forward pass.

∑ The math (paper §4, standard network)

The paper writes the forward pass of a standard network, for hidden layers \(l \in \{0, \dots, L-1\}\) and any unit \(i\):

\[ z_i^{(l+1)} = \mathbf{w}_i^{(l+1)}\, \mathbf{y}^{(l)} + b_i^{(l+1)}, \qquad y_i^{(l+1)} = f\big(z_i^{(l+1)}\big). \]

Read it aloud: “the input \(z\) of unit \(i\) in layer \(l+1\) is its weight vector times the outputs of layer \(l\), plus its bias; its output \(y\) is \(f\) of that”. The superscript \((l)\) is a layer number, not a power. \(\mathbf{y}^{(l)}\) is the vector of all outputs of layer \(l\), with \(\mathbf{y}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}\), the input. \(\mathbf{w}_i^{(l+1)}\) is the row of weights belonging to unit \(i\); multiplying a row by a vector means multiplying entry by entry and adding up (a dot product). \(b_i^{(l+1)}\) is the unit’s bias and \(f\) its activation function. \(L\) is the number of hidden layers. Example: \(\mathbf{w} = (0.9, -0.4, 0.3)\), \(\mathbf{y} = (1, 0.5, -0.5)\), \(b = 0.1\): \(z = 0.9 - 0.2 - 0.15 + 0.1 = 0.65\), and the logistic \(f(0.65) = 0.657\). That is unit 1 in the widget above.

📄 In the paper

Section 4 sets out this notation before adding dropout to it, and names the logistic function as its example of \(f\) Paper §4. Most results use ReLUs; Table 2 shows that switching from logistic units to ReLUs lowers MNIST error from 1.35 % to 1.25 % Paper Table 2.

Foundations

How a network learns

Measure how wrong the network is with a loss, find which way each weight should move with backpropagation, and take small steps downhill.

At first the weights are random and the outputs are nonsense. To improve them we need a single number that says how wrong the network is: the loss. For classification the usual loss is the cross-entropy: the output layer turns its scores into probabilities with a softmax (exponentiate each score, divide by the total), and the loss is \(-\log\) of the probability given to the correct class. It is 0 when the network is certain and right, and large when it is confident and wrong.

Learning means changing the weights to make the loss smaller. The gradient says, for every weight, how much the loss would rise if that weight grew a little. Gradient descent moves every weight a small step the other way, of size learning rate × gradient:

How do we get the gradient for a weight deep inside the network? By the chain rule of calculus, applied backwards from the loss through each layer: backpropagation. The widget below runs it on the smallest possible network so every number is visible.

∑ The math

The chain rule says that if the loss \(L\) depends on \(w_2\) only through \(\hat y\), and \(\hat y\) through \(z_2 = w_2 h\), then

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y}\cdot\frac{\partial \hat y}{\partial z_2}\cdot\frac{\partial z_2}{\partial w_2} = (\hat y - t)\cdot \hat y(1-\hat y)\cdot h, \qquad w \leftarrow w - \varepsilon\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}. \]

\(\partial L/\partial w\) (“the partial derivative of \(L\) with respect to \(w\)”) is the slope of the loss when only \(w\) changes. \(t\) is the target, \(\hat y\) the prediction, and \(\hat y(1-\hat y)\) is the slope of the logistic function. \(\varepsilon\) is the learning rate. For \(w_1\), one more factor joins the chain: the error is sent back through \(w_2\) and through the slope \(h(1-h)\) of the hidden unit. A real network does the same for millions of weights at once.

Computing the gradient on the whole training set for every step is slow. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) uses a minibatch: a small random handful of training cases, whose gradients are averaged. One pass over the whole training set is an epoch. Here a network with 8 hidden units learns to separate two classes of points; the shading is its current prediction:

Foundations

Overfitting

A big network can learn its training examples perfectly, noise and all, and then do worse on new data.

We do not care how well a network does on the examples it learned from; we care how well it does on new ones. So data is split: a training set to learn from, and a test set, never shown during training, to measure generalization. The error is the percentage of examples classified wrongly.

The paper’s first paragraph explains the danger. Deep networks are very expressive, but “with limited training data, however, many of these complicated relationships will be the result of sampling noise”: accidents of which examples happened to be collected. They exist in the training set but not in new data. Learning them is overfitting.

The oldest defence is early stopping: keep a third set of examples, the validation set, check its error during training, and keep the weights from the moment it was lowest. The validation set is also how people choose hyperparameters (settings such as the learning rate) without peeking at the test set.

📄 In the paper

The introduction lists the classic remedies: “stopping the training as soon as performance on a validation set starts to get worse”, weight penalties such as L1 and L2, and soft weight sharing Paper §1. In the MNIST experiments the hyperparameters were tuned on 10,000 held-out training images, and they were chosen so that “early stopping was not required at all” Paper App. B.1.

💡 Intuition

A student who memorises last year’s exam answers scores 100 % on last year’s exam and fails this year’s. Overfitting is memorising instead of understanding. Dropout, as we will see, makes memorising hard.

Foundations

Weight penalties

Add the size of the weights to the loss, and the network prefers simple, smooth solutions.

Wild, overfitted fits usually need large weights. So a classic remedy, called regularization, adds a penalty for large weights to the loss. With the L2 penalty (also weight decay, Tikhonov regularization or, for linear models, ridge regression) the penalty is \(\lambda\) times the sum of squared weights. Its strength \(\lambda\) is a hyperparameter.

The L1 penalty (the lasso for linear models) adds \(\lambda\) times the sum of absolute values instead. The two behave differently: L2 shrinks every weight a little, L1 pushes small weights all the way to zero.

∑ The math
\[ \text{L2: } \; \text{loss}(\mathbf w) + \lambda \lVert \mathbf w\rVert_2^2 = \text{loss}(\mathbf w) + \lambda \sum_i w_i^2, \qquad \text{L1: } \; \text{loss}(\mathbf w) + \lambda \sum_i \lvert w_i\rvert. \]

\(\lVert \mathbf w \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i w_i^2}\) is the length (the L2 norm) of the weight vector. For linear regression with data matrix \(X\) and targets \(\mathbf y\), the L2-penalised problem \(\min_{\mathbf w} \lVert \mathbf y - X\mathbf w\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert \mathbf w\rVert^2\) has the exact solution \(\mathbf w = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf y\), which is what the first widget computes. Remember this form: in Chapter 28 dropout turns out to be exactly such a penalty, with a twist.

📄 In the paper

Section 6.5 compares dropout with “L2 weight decay (more generally Tikhonov regularization (Tikhonov, 1943)), lasso (Tibshirani, 1996), KL-sparsity and max-norm regularization” on one MNIST network Paper §6.5, Table 9. Deepnet implements both penalties inside the weight update, as apply_l2_decay and apply_l1_decay parameter.py Update.

Foundations

Combining many models

The average prediction of several different models is usually better than any one of them, but averaging big networks is expensive.

Ask many people to guess the weight of an ox and average their guesses: the average is often better than almost every individual guess, because their errors partly cancel. Machine learning uses the same trick, called model combination or an ensemble: train several models and average their predicted probabilities.

Averaging only helps if the models make different mistakes. For neural networks that means different architectures or different training data, and the paper points out why both are painful: each architecture needs its own hyperparameter search, big networks need lots of data, and at test time every model must be run, which is too slow when answers are needed quickly.

There are two natural ways to average probabilities. The arithmetic mean adds them and divides by the number of models. The geometric mean multiplies them and takes the root; it must then be renormalised (divided by its total) so the class probabilities add to 1 again.

∑ The math
\[ \text{arithmetic: } \bar P(c) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} P_k(c), \qquad \text{normalised geometric: } \tilde P(c) = \frac{\big(\prod_{k=1}^{K} P_k(c)\big)^{1/K}}{\sum_{c'} \big(\prod_{k=1}^{K} P_k(c')\big)^{1/K}}. \]

\(P_k(c)\) is model \(k\)’s probability for class \(c\), and \(K\) is the number of models. \(\prod\) means “multiply together”. Taking logs turns the geometric mean into an average of log-probabilities, which is why it fits so naturally with softmax outputs (Chapter 12).

The ideal average, the paper says, is Bayesian: average the predictions of all possible settings of the weights, each weighted by its posterior probability, i.e. how plausible it is given the training data. That is the “gold standard”, but it is only feasible for simple or small models.

📄 In the paper

“With unlimited computation, the best way to ‘regularize’ a fixed-sized model is to average the predictions of all possible settings of the parameters, weighting each setting by its posterior probability given the training data.” The authors want to approach this Bayesian gold standard with much less computation, “by approximating an equally weighted geometric mean of the predictions of an exponential number of learned models that share parameters” Paper §1. Section 6.4 later compares dropout with a real Bayesian neural network.

Foundations

Coin flips and averages

Dropout is driven by biased coins; their averages behave in simple, exact ways.

A random variable is a number decided by chance. The simplest is a Bernoulli variable with parameter \(p\): it is 1 with probability \(p\) and 0 otherwise, like a coin that lands heads a fraction \(p\) of the time. We write \(r \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)\), “r is drawn from Bernoulli(p)”.

Two summaries describe a random variable. Its expected value \(E[r]\) is its long-run average. Its variance \(\mathrm{Var}[r]\) is the average squared distance from that average: how much it wobbles. A Gaussian (normal) variable \(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) is the familiar bell curve with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) (\(\sigma\) is its standard deviation).

∑ The math
\[ r \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p): \quad E[r] = 1\cdot p + 0\cdot(1-p) = p, \qquad \mathrm{Var}[r] = E[(r - p)^2] = p(1-p). \]

Two rules make dropout analysis easy. Linearity of expectation: the average of a sum is the sum of the averages, \(E[\sum_j a_j r_j] = \sum_j a_j E[r_j]\), for any fixed numbers \(a_j\). Independence: for independent \(r_j\), variances add: \(\mathrm{Var}[\sum_j a_j r_j] = \sum_j a_j^2\,\mathrm{Var}[r_j]\). Example: with \(p = 0.5\), \(\mathrm{Var}[r] = 0.25\); for the value \(1/p\) instead of 1 (used in Chapter 29) the mean becomes 1 and the variance \((1-p)/p\).

Convolutional nets in one chapter

For images, small filters slide over the picture and pooling summarises what they find; most parameters end up in the fully connected layers on top.

The networks so far are fully connected: every unit sees every input. For images there is a better design. A convolutional layer learns small filters (for example 5×5 pixels) and applies each one at every position of the image. Each filter produces a feature map: how strongly the pattern it looks for (an edge, a corner, a blob) appears at each place. Using the same few weights everywhere is called weight sharing. A max-pooling layer then keeps only the largest value in each small window, which makes the result smaller and less sensitive to small shifts.

A typical convolutional neural network (conv net, LeCun et al., 1989) stacks a few convolution + pooling stages and finishes with fully connected layers that make the decision. The paper’s best network for house-number photos is exactly that:

The convolutional layers are followed by two fully connected hidden layers having 2048 units each”, all ReLU Paper App. B.2. The ImageNet network is that of Krizhevsky et al. (2012) Paper §6.1.4.

The dropout model

The big idea

For every training case, delete a random subset of units; at test time, use the whole network with its weights scaled down.

Here is the paper’s whole idea in one sentence: dropout means temporarily removing units from the network, “along with all its incoming and outgoing connections”, at random, independently for every presentation of every training case. In the simplest case each unit is kept (“retained”) with a fixed probability \(p\), independently of all others. The network that survives is called a thinned network.

Count them: a network with \(n\) droppable units has \(2^n\) possible thinned networks, because each unit is either in or out. They all share the same weights, so the number of parameters does not grow. Training with dropout therefore “can be seen as training a collection of \(2^n\) thinned networks with extensive weight sharing, where each thinned network gets trained very rarely, if at all”.

At test time, running all \(2^n\) networks and averaging them is impossible. Instead the paper uses one network without dropout whose outgoing weights are multiplied by \(p\). Chapter 11 shows why this matches each unit’s expected output, and Chapter 12 why it approximates averaging all the thinned networks. Does it work? These networks all share weights so that the total number of parameters is still \(O(n^2)\), or less.” The retention probability “can simply be set at 0.5, which seems to be close to optimal for a wide range of networks and tasks. 💡 Intuition

Imagine a team where, every day, a random half of the members stay home. Nobody can count on a particular colleague to fix their mistakes, so everyone learns to do something useful alone. That is the next chapter.

The dropout model

Why it works: co-adaptation

Units that cannot rely on specific partners must each learn something useful on their own.

The paper’s motivation comes from biology. Sexual reproduction takes half the genes of one parent and half of the other, which breaks up sets of genes that had evolved to work well together. That seems wasteful, yet most advanced organisms reproduce sexually. One explanation (Livnat et al., 2010) is that natural selection rewards mix-ability: a gene that works well with many random partners is more robust than one that only works inside a fixed team.

Translated to networks: during ordinary training, each weight is adjusted to reduce the loss “given what all other units are doing”, so units can learn to fix up each other’s mistakes. These delicate team arrangements are called co-adaptations. They fit the training set and often fail on new data. With dropout, any partner may be missing, so co-adaptations stop paying off.

Can we see the difference in trained networks? A direct test: remove units after training and see how badly each network suffers.

📄 In the paper

Section 2 gives both stories. Genes: “the role of sexual reproduction is not just to allow useful new genes to spread throughout the population, but also to facilitate this process by reducing complex co-adaptations”. Networks: “each hidden unit in a neural network trained with dropout must learn to work with a randomly chosen sample of other units”. Conspiracies: “Ten conspiracies each involving five people is probably a better way to create havoc than one big conspiracy that requires fifty people to all play their parts correctly” Paper §2. Section 7.1 returns to the idea with pictures of learned features (Chapter 22).

⚠ Careful

Could the network beat dropout by keeping many copies of each unit? The paper says this is “a poor solution for exactly the same reason as replica codes are a poor way to deal with a noisy channel”: copies waste capacity. The units of a layer still learn different things. The knockout widget is the book’s own experiment, not one of the paper’s.

The dropout model

The dropout forward pass

Before a layer’s outputs are passed on, multiply each one by its own random 0-or-1 gate.

Section 4 adds just one step to the standard forward pass of Chapter 1. Each output \(y_j^{(l)}\) of layer \(l\) gets its own gate \(r_j^{(l)}\), a Bernoulli(\(p\)) coin. The thinned outputs \(\tilde y_j^{(l)} = r_j^{(l)} y_j^{(l)}\) are what the next layer reads. A gate of 0 deletes the unit for this case.

∑ The math (paper §4, dropout network)
\[ r_j^{(l)} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p), \qquad \tilde{\mathbf y}^{(l)} = \mathbf r^{(l)} * \mathbf y^{(l)}, \qquad z_i^{(l+1)} = \mathbf w_i^{(l+1)}\,\tilde{\mathbf y}^{(l)} + b_i^{(l+1)}, \qquad y_i^{(l+1)} = f\big(z_i^{(l+1)}\big). \]

\(\mathbf r^{(l)}\) is a vector of independent Bernoulli variables, one per unit of layer \(l\). \(*\) is the element-wise product: multiply the two vectors entry by entry (not a dot product). So \(\tilde{\mathbf y}^{(l)}\) equals \(\mathbf y^{(l)}\) with some entries set to 0. The last two lines are the standard network, reading \(\tilde{\mathbf y}\) instead of \(\mathbf y\). This is applied at every layer, with its own \(p\) per layer if wanted. Example from the widget: \(\mathbf y = (0.9, 0.2, 0.7)\), \(\mathbf r = (1, 0, 1)\) give \(\tilde{\mathbf y} = (0.9, 0, 0.7)\), and \(z = 1.2\cdot0.9 + 0.6\cdot0.7 - 0.3 = 1.2\).

The dropout model

Test time: shrink the weights

At test time every unit is present, so its outgoing weights are multiplied by p to give the next layer the input it saw on average during training.

After training we want one fixed network that gives repeatable answers. If we simply switched dropout off, each unit would suddenly receive about \(1/p\) times more input than it was used to (twice as much for \(p = 0.5\)). The fix (Fig. 2): keep every unit, but multiply its outgoing weights by \(p\).

∑ The math (paper §4)
\[ W^{(l)}_{test} = p\,W^{(l)}, \qquad\text{because}\qquad E\Big[\sum_j w_{ij}\, r_j\, y_j\Big] = \sum_j w_{ij}\, E[r_j]\, y_j = \sum_j (p\, w_{ij})\, y_j. \]

The left side is the average input a unit received during training (over the random gates \(r_j\), with the outputs \(y_j\) held fixed); the right side is what it receives at test time with weights \(p\,w_{ij}\). They are equal by linearity of expectation (Chapter 6). For the input \(z\) this is exact. For the output \(f(z)\) it is only approximate, because \(f\) is curved and the average of \(f(z)\) is not \(f\) of the average; the widget’s right panel shows the small gap.

There is a second way to do the same bookkeeping. Section 10 mentions it in passing: instead of shrinking weights at test time, scale up the kept units by \(1/p\) during training and leave test time alone. This inverted dropout is what most libraries do, including the one that trains this book’s toy networks:

📄 In the paper

“If a unit is retained with probability \(p\) during training, the outgoing weights of that unit are multiplied by \(p\) at test time as shown in Figure 2. This ensures that for any hidden unit the expected output (under the distribution used to drop units at training time) is the same as the actual output at test time” Paper §1, Fig. 2, §4. The inverted form: “scale up the retained activations by multiplying by \(1/p\) at training time and not modifying the weights at test time. These methods are equivalent with appropriate scaling of the learning rate and weight initializations at each layer” Paper §10.

⌨ In the code

The shared library of this collection uses the inverted form (NN.dropout multiplies kept entries by \(1/p\)).

Weight scaling as model averaging

The one weight-scaled network is a cheap stand-in for averaging the predictions of all 2ⁿ thinned networks.

Training with dropout trains \(2^n\) weight-sharing networks. The “right” prediction is then an average over all of them, which Chapter 5 taught us is usually better than any single model. The paper claims that the weight-scaled network approximates this average. For a tiny network we can check, by running every thinned network:

Which average does weight scaling approximate? The introduction says “an equally weighted geometric mean”. For the very last layer the match is not approximate at all:

∑ The math (the book’s derivation)

Take a softmax output layer with weights \(W\) reading inputs \(\mathbf x\) that are dropped with gates \(\mathbf r\). Each thinned model predicts \(P_{\mathbf r}(c) \propto \exp(s_c(\mathbf r))\) with scores \(s_c(\mathbf r) = \sum_j w_{jc} r_j x_j + b_c\). The geometric mean, weighted by how likely each mask is, has logarithm

\[ \sum_{\mathbf r} P(\mathbf r)\,\log P_{\mathbf r}(c) = \sum_{\mathbf r} P(\mathbf r)\, s_c(\mathbf r) - (\text{terms that do not depend on } c) = \sum_j (p\,w_{jc})\, x_j + b_c - \text{const}. \]

After renormalising, the constant cancels and we get exactly \(\text{softmax}\big((pW)^\top \mathbf x + \mathbf b\big)\): the weight-scaled model. The middle step is linearity of expectation again, \(E[r_j] = p\). With hidden layers in between, the non-linear activations break this exactness, but the first widget shows that the approximation stays close. (With \(p = 0.5\) all masks are equally likely, so the weighting by \(P(\mathbf r)\) is the paper’s “equally weighted” mean. This derivation is the book’s; the paper states the conclusion without it.)

📄 In the paper

“By doing this scaling, \(2^n\) networks with shared weights can be combined into a single neural network to be used at test time” Paper §1. Dropout is “a way of doing an equally-weighted averaging of exponentially many models with shared weights” Paper §6.4. Section 7.5 tests the approximation against Monte-Carlo averaging on MNIST (Chapter 25).

The dropout model

Training a thinned network

Backpropagate through the thinned network only; weights of dropped units get zero gradient from that case.

Training needs almost no change. For each training case in a minibatch, sample a thinned network, run the forward pass and backpropagation “only on this thinned network”, then average the gradients over the minibatch as usual. A dropped unit’s output was multiplied by 0, so by the chain rule every gradient that flows through it is multiplied by 0 too: its incoming and outgoing weights learn nothing from that case.

∑ The math
\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial y_j^{(l)}} = r_j^{(l)}\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial \tilde y_j^{(l)}}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ij}^{(l+1)}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i^{(l+1)}}\,\tilde y_j^{(l)}. \]

Both carry a factor \(r_j^{(l)}\): if the gate is 0, unit \(j\) passes back no error (left) and its outgoing weights see input 0 (right). The minibatch gradient is the average of these per-case gradients, so a weight’s update comes only from the cases that kept its unit. “Any training case which does not use a parameter contributes a gradient of zero for that parameter.”

📄 In the paper

“The only difference is that for each training case in a mini-batch, we sample a thinned network by dropping out units. Forward and backpropagation for that training case are done only on this thinned network. The gradients for each parameter are averaged over the training cases in each mini-batch.” Momentum, annealed learning rates and L2 weight decay “were found to be useful for dropout neural networks as well” Paper §5.1.

⌨ In the code

Deepnet never builds a separate thinned network. The mask zeroes the stored state of dropped units, and the ReLU layer’s backward step multiplies the incoming error by the ReLU slope computed from that same state (self.deriv.apply_rectified_linear_deriv(self.state)), which is 0 for zeroed units relu_layer.py l.28–30, neuralnet.py ComputeDown. The book’s widget uses the shared autograd, where the mask enters as an ordinary multiplication.

The dropout model

Max-norm, big steps and momentum

Cap each unit’s weight vector, then take large, momentum-driven, gradually shrinking steps.

Dropout makes gradients very noisy: each step trains a different random network. The paper found a combination that works especially well with it. The first ingredient is max-norm regularization: limit the length of the vector of incoming weights of every hidden unit to at most a constant \(c\). Whenever an update pushes the vector outside the ball of radius \(c\), project it back onto the ball’s surface.

∑ The math (paper §5.1)
\[ \lVert \mathbf w \rVert_2 \le c, \qquad \text{after each update:}\quad \mathbf w \leftarrow \begin{cases} \mathbf w & \text{if } \lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2 \le c\\ c\,\mathbf w / \lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases} \]

\(\mathbf w\) is the vector of weights coming into one hidden unit, \(\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2\) its length. Unlike an L2 penalty, which always pulls weights towards 0, max-norm does nothing until the limit is reached, then stops the weights from growing further, whatever the learning rate.

The second ingredient is momentum: instead of stepping along the current gradient alone, keep a running “velocity” that remembers recent steps. It smooths out noisy gradients and speeds progress along long, gentle valleys.

The third is a large learning rate that decays over time. Early, big noisy steps explore; later, small steps settle into a minimum. Deepnet ramps the momentum up at the same time:

∑ The math (momentum and decay, as in deepnet and App. B.4)
\[ \mathbf v \leftarrow \mu\,\mathbf v + \nabla L, \qquad \mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - \varepsilon_t\,\mathbf v, \qquad \varepsilon_t = \varepsilon_0\,(1 + t/T)^{-1}. \]

\(\mathbf v\) is the velocity, \(\mu\) the momentum (0 = plain SGD, close to 1 = long memory), \(\nabla L\) the minibatch gradient, \(\varepsilon_t\) the learning rate at step \(t\), \(\varepsilon_0\) its starting value and \(T\) the step count at which it has halved.

📄 In the paper

Max-norm, “previously used in the context of collaborative filtering (Srebro and Shraibman, 2005)”, “typically improves the performance of stochastic gradient descent training of deep neural nets, even when no dropout is used”. With dropout, “max-norm regularization, large decaying learning rates and high momentum provides a significant boost over just using dropout”. The proposed reason: the ball “makes it possible to use a huge learning rate without the possibility of weights blowing up”, and the dropout noise lets the optimizer explore regions it would not otherwise reach; as the learning rate decays it settles Paper §5.1. Values used: \(c = 2\) on MNIST, \(c = 4\) on SVHN and TIMIT; typical \(c\) is 3 to 4; momentum 0.95 to 0.99; learning rate 10 to 100 times that of a standard net; on TIMIT \(\varepsilon_0 = 0.1\) with decay \(\varepsilon_0(1 + t/T)^{-1}\) Paper App. A.2–A.3, B.1–B.4.

⌨ In the code

The update, then the projection: w_delta.mult(momentum); …; w_delta.add_mult(gradient); w.add_mult(w_delta, -epsilon); if h.apply_weight_norm: w.norm_limit(h.weight_norm, axis=0). The projection works column by column, one column per receiving unit parameter.py l.80–106. Schedules: momentum \(= \mu_{final} - (\mu_{final} - \mu_{initial})\,e^{-t/\tau}\), and learning rate base_epsilon / (1 + step / epsilon_decay_half_life) parameter.py l.108–125. The dropout MNIST example uses weight_norm: 3, base_epsilon: 1.0, half-life 10,000, momentum 0.5 → 0.99 model_dropout.pbtxt.

The dropout model

Pretraining, then dropout finetuning

Weights learned without labels can start a dropout network, if they are scaled up by 1/p and finetuned gently.

Labelled examples are expensive; unlabelled ones are cheap. Unsupervised pretraining first trains layers on unlabelled data to model the inputs, then uses those weights to start a classifier, which is then finetuned with labels by backpropagation. The paper mentions three ways: stacks of RBMs (a Deep Belief Net, DBN; RBMs are explained in Chapter 26), autoencoders, which learn to reproduce their input through a hidden layer, and Deep Boltzmann Machines (DBM).

Seven data sets and how to score them

The paper tests dropout on images, speech, text and genetics, to show it is a general technique, not a trick for one field.

The authors trained dropout networks on classification problems from four domains and report that “dropout improved generalization performance on all data sets compared to neural networks that did not use dropout”. Here are the data sets:

  • SVHN (Street View House Numbers): 32×32 colour photos of digits on house fronts, from Google Street View.

Dropout helps speech recognition a lot, news classification a little, and on a tiny genetics data set it comes second only to a Bayesian network.

A document is turned into a bag of words: how often each of the 2000 vocabulary words occurs, ignoring order. From over 800,000 Reuters articles in 103 topic classes the authors built a subset of 402,738 articles in 50 topics, each in exactly one, split into equal training and test halves. Genetics: dropout vs a Bayesian neural network

A Bayesian neural network (Neal, 1996) does the proper averaging of Chapter 5: over network weights, weighting each by how well it fits the data and a prior. Dropout weights all its thinned networks equally. Bayesian nets are excellent when data is scarce, but slow to train and hard to scale. The test: predicting alternative splicing (which pieces of a gene’s RNA are kept, depending on the tissue) from 1014 RNA features of 3665 cassette exons, in 4 tissue types derived from 27 mouse tissues. For each exon and tissue the target is a distribution over 3 states (inc, exc, nc), and the score is code quality:

∑ The math (paper App. B.6)
\[ \text{Code quality} = \sum_{i=1}^{|\text{data points}|}\ \sum_{t \in \text{tissue types}}\ \sum_{s \in \{inc,\,exc,\,nc\}} p^s_{i,t}\,\log\!\left(\frac{q^s_t(r_i)}{\bar p^{\,s}}\right). \]

\(p^s_{i,t}\) is the target probability (from lab experiments) of state \(s\) for exon \(i\) in tissue \(t\); \(q^s_t(r_i)\) is the model’s predicted probability given the exon’s features \(r_i\); \(\bar p^{\,s}\) is the average of \(p^s_{i,t}\) over all \(i\) and \(t\). Each term rewards predicting more probability than the average where the target puts its mass. It equals “bits gained over always predicting the average” minus the KL divergence \(\sum_s p^s \log(p^s/q^s)\), the standard measure of how far a predicted distribution is from a target one; that is the paper’s “negative KL divergence between the target and the predicted probability distributions (higher is better)” Paper §6.4, App. B.6. The unit “bits” means the log is taken in base 2.

📄 In the paper

“As expected, we found that Bayesian neural nets perform better than dropout. However, we see that dropout improves significantly upon the performance of standard neural nets and outperforms all other methods.” Other methods fight overfitting by shrinking the 1014 inputs with PCA first; dropout did not need that. “The dropout nets are very large (1000s of hidden units) compared to a few tens of units in the Bayesian network.” Setup: two layers of 1024 units, \(p = 0.5\) hidden and 0.7 input, max-norm with high decaying learning rates, the same 5 folds as Xiong et al. (2011) Paper §6.4, Table 8, App. B.6.

⚠ Careful

Table 7 lists 20.7 % for the 8-layer DBN-pretrained net without dropout, but the text says the 8-layer error “reduces from 20.5 % to 19.7 %”; 20.5 % is the 5-layer mcRBM row. The widget shows the table’s values.

Against other regularizers

On one fixed network, dropout with max-norm beats L2, L1, KL-sparsity and max-norm alone.

The cleanest comparison in the paper: one architecture, 784-1024-1024-2048-10 with ReLUs, trained on MNIST with SGD and a different regularizer each time, each tuned on a validation set. Features and sparsity

With dropout, each hidden unit learns a pattern that means something on its own, and only a few units are active at a time.

Section 7 looks inside trained networks to test the co-adaptation story of Chapter 9. The simplest place to look is an autoencoder: a network that squeezes its input through one hidden layer and tries to reproduce it at the output. The incoming weights of each hidden unit are a feature: the input pattern that excites it most, which can be drawn as an image.

Inside a dropout network

Monte-Carlo averaging vs weight scaling

Averaging about 50 random thinned networks matches the single weight-scaled network; weight scaling is a good approximation at a fiftieth of the cost.

Chapter 12 checked weight scaling by running every thinned network of a tiny net. For real networks there are far too many. The practical alternative is Monte-Carlo model averaging: for each test case, sample \(k\) thinned networks with the training-time dropout, run each, and average their predicted probabilities. As \(k\) grows this approaches the true average over all thinned networks.

∑ The math
\[ \hat P_k(c \mid \mathbf x) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{k} P\big(c \mid \mathbf x;\ \mathbf r^{[s]}\big), \qquad \mathbf r^{[s]} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p) \text{ independently}, \qquad \hat P_k \xrightarrow{\;k\to\infty\;} E_{\mathbf r}\big[P(c\mid\mathbf x;\mathbf r)\big]. \]

\(P(c \mid \mathbf x; \mathbf r^{[s]})\) is the prediction of the thinned network picked by the \(s\)-th random mask. The limit is the arithmetic average over all thinned networks, each weighted by its probability. The cost grows linearly with \(k\).

📄 In the paper

On MNIST, “It can be seen that around \(k = 50\), the Monte-Carlo method becomes as good as the approximate method. Thereafter, the Monte-Carlo method is slightly better than the approximate method but well within one standard deviation of it. This suggests that the weight scaling method is a fairly good approximation of the true model average” Paper §7.5, Fig. 11. In Fig. 11 the weight-scaling error is about 1.05 % and Monte-Carlo starts near 1.3 % for one or two samples (read off, approximate).

⌨ In the code

Deepnet has a script for exactly this experiment: it writes the output-layer predictions of many dropout samples to disk (WriteRepresentationToDisk(…, drop=True)), then averages random subsets of \(k\) of them and reports the mean and standard deviation of the error over repeats mc_avg.py.

Inside a dropout network

Restricted Boltzmann machines

An RBM gives every on/off pattern of its units an energy, and learns to make the patterns in the data likely.

Dropout is not only for feed-forward networks. Section 8 applies it to Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs), the building block of the DBN pretraining used in Tables 2 and 7. An RBM has a layer of visible units \(\mathbf v\) (for example the pixels of a binarised image, each 0 or 1) and a layer of hidden units \(\mathbf h\), also 0 or 1. Every visible unit is connected to every hidden unit, and there are no connections within a layer: that is the “restricted”. Instead of computing an output, an RBM defines a probability for every joint pattern \((\mathbf v, \mathbf h)\).

∑ The math (paper §8.1)
\[ P(\mathbf h, \mathbf v; \theta) = \frac{1}{\mathcal Z(\theta)} \exp\big(\mathbf v^\top W \mathbf h + \mathbf a^\top \mathbf h + \mathbf b^\top \mathbf v\big), \qquad \mathbf v \in \{0,1\}^D,\ \mathbf h \in \{0,1\}^F. \]

\(D\) and \(F\) are the numbers of visible and hidden units, \(\theta = \{W, \mathbf a, \mathbf b\}\) the parameters: weights \(W\) (\(D\times F\)) and biases \(\mathbf a\) (hidden) and \(\mathbf b\) (visible). The exponent is minus the energy \(E(\mathbf v, \mathbf h)\): low energy, high probability. \(\mathcal Z(\theta)\), the partition function, adds \(\exp(-E)\) over all \(2^{D+F}\) patterns so that the probabilities sum to 1. Because hidden units do not talk to each other, given \(\mathbf v\) they are independent, each switching on with a logistic probability:

\[ P(h_j = 1 \mid \mathbf v) = \sigma\Big(a_j + \sum_i W_{ij} v_i\Big), \qquad P(v_i = 1 \mid \mathbf h) = \sigma\Big(b_i + \sum_j W_{ij} h_j\Big), \]

where \(\sigma\) is the logistic function. (The paper writes the hidden bias as \(b_j\) and the visible bias as \(a_i\) in these two conditionals, swapping the letters of its energy formula; the book keeps one convention throughout.)

Training an RBM means raising the probability of the training patterns, which requires lowering the energy at the data and raising it elsewhere. The exact gradient needs \(\mathcal Z\), which is impossible to compute for real sizes, so RBMs are trained with contrastive divergence (Hinton et al., 2006). CD-1, the one-step version used in the paper, compares the data with a one-step reconstruction:

∑ The math (CD-1, background the paper cites)
\[ \Delta W_{ij} = \varepsilon\big(v_i\,P(h_j{=}1\mid\mathbf v) - v'_i\,P(h_j{=}1\mid\mathbf v')\big), \qquad \mathbf h \sim P(\mathbf h\mid\mathbf v),\ \ \mathbf v' \sim P(\mathbf v\mid\mathbf h). \]

The first term (the “positive phase”) strengthens connections active on the data; the second (the “negative phase”) weakens those active on the model’s own reconstruction \(\mathbf v'\). Biases are updated the same way. The toy uses probabilities instead of samples for \(\mathbf v'\), a common choice.

📄 In the paper

The RBM definition is in §8.1; “Learning algorithms developed for RBMs such as Contrastive Divergence (Hinton et al., 2006) can be directly applied for learning Dropout RBMs … In our experiments, we use CD-1” Paper §8.1–8.2. A Deep Belief Net stacks RBMs, each trained on the hidden activities of the one below; a Deep Boltzmann Machine trains all layers jointly. The TIMIT RBMs were trained with CD-1, with Gaussian input units of variance 1 Paper App. B.4.

Inside a dropout network

Dropout RBMs

Gate every hidden unit of an RBM with a random coin: the model becomes a mixture of exponentially many RBMs sharing weights.

A dropout RBM adds a vector of binary gates \(\mathbf r\), one per hidden unit, each 1 with probability \(p\). A gate of 0 removes its hidden unit: that unit is forced to be 0.

∑ The math (paper §8.1)
\[ P(\mathbf r, \mathbf h, \mathbf v; p, \theta) = P(\mathbf r; p)\,P(\mathbf h, \mathbf v \mid \mathbf r; \theta), \qquad P(\mathbf r; p) = \prod_{j=1}^{F} p^{r_j}(1-p)^{1-r_j}, \] \[ P(\mathbf h, \mathbf v \mid \mathbf r; \theta) = \frac{1}{\mathcal Z'(\theta, \mathbf r)} \exp\big(\mathbf v^\top W\mathbf h + \mathbf a^\top\mathbf h + \mathbf b^\top\mathbf v\big) \prod_{j=1}^{F} g(h_j, r_j), \qquad g(h_j, r_j) = \mathbb 1(r_j = 1) + \mathbb 1(r_j = 0)\,\mathbb 1(h_j = 0). \]

\(P(\mathbf r; p)\) is the probability of a gate pattern: each gate contributes \(p\) if it is 1 and \(1 - p\) if it is 0. \(\mathbb 1(\cdot)\) is the indicator: 1 if the condition is true, 0 otherwise. So \(g(h_j, r_j) = 1\) whenever the gate is open, and when it is closed (\(r_j = 0\)) it is 1 only if \(h_j = 0\): a closed gate makes every pattern with \(h_j = 1\) impossible. \(\mathcal Z'(\theta, \mathbf r)\) normalises for each \(\mathbf r\). The conditionals become

\[ P(\mathbf h \mid \mathbf r, \mathbf v) = \prod_{j=1}^{F} P(h_j \mid r_j, \mathbf v), \qquad P(h_j = 1 \mid r_j, \mathbf v) = \mathbb 1(r_j = 1)\,\sigma\Big(a_j + \sum_i W_{ij} v_i\Big), \qquad P(\mathbf v \mid \mathbf h) = \prod_{i=1}^{D} P(v_i \mid \mathbf h), \]

with \(P(v_i = 1 \mid \mathbf h)\) unchanged from Chapter 26. (Again the book writes \(a_j\) for the hidden bias where the paper writes \(b_j\).) The “Drop h₂” button in the previous chapter’s widget is exactly \(r_2 = 0\).

For a fixed \(\mathbf r\), the model is an ordinary RBM with some hidden units removed. So a dropout RBM is a mixture of \(2^F\) RBMs with shared weights, the same structure as a dropout network. Learning is CD-1 with one change: draw a fresh \(\mathbf r\) for every training case, and use only the retained hidden units.

📄 In the paper

“Conditioned on \(\mathbf r\), the distribution over \(\{\mathbf v, \mathbf h\}\) is same as the distribution that an RBM would impose, except that the units for which \(r_j = 0\) are dropped from \(\mathbf h\). Therefore, the Dropout RBM model can be seen as a mixture of exponentially many RBMs with shared weights each using a different subset of \(\mathbf h\)” Paper §8.1. With 256 hidden units on MNIST, the dropout RBM’s features “seem to capture features that are coarser compared to the sharply defined stroke-like features in the standard RBM. There seem to be very few dead units in the dropout RBM relative to the standard RBM” Paper §8.3, Fig. 12, and “dropout RBMs learn much sparser representations than standard RBMs even when no additional sparsity inducing regularizer is present” Paper §8.4, Fig. 13.

⌨ In the code

In deepnet’s Boltzmann-machine code the mask is drawn only in the positive phase (if not neg_phase: layer.mask.fill_with_rand(); …) and reused in the negative phase, so a training case keeps the same \(\mathbf r\) in both halves of CD; when not training, states are multiplied by \(p\) as in feed-forward nets dbm.py l.106–115. The toy RBM does the same.

Beyond Bernoulli noise

Marginalising the noise

For linear regression, dropout on the inputs is exactly ridge regression with a penalty that grows as p shrinks.

Dropout minimises the loss “stochastically under a noise distribution”: every step sees a different random mask. What objective is it really minimising? Its average over all masks, the expected loss. Computing that average in closed form is called marginalising the noise. It is hard for deep networks, but for linear regression (predict a number \(y\) as a weighted sum \(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf w\) of the inputs, by least squares) it can be done exactly.

∑ The math (paper §9.1)

Data matrix \(X \in \mathbb R^{N\times D}\) (\(N\) data points as rows, \(D\) features as columns), targets \(\mathbf y \in \mathbb R^{N}\), weights \(\mathbf w \in \mathbb R^{D}\). Plain linear regression minimises \(\lVert \mathbf y - X\mathbf w \rVert^2\). Dropout on the inputs multiplies \(X\) element-wise by a random matrix \(R \in \{0,1\}^{N\times D}\), \(R_{ij} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)\). The marginalised objective and what it reduces to:

\[ \underset{\mathbf w}{\text{minimize}}\ \ \mathbb E_{R\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)}\big[\lVert \mathbf y - (R * X)\mathbf w\rVert^2\big] \;=\; \underset{\mathbf w}{\text{minimize}}\ \ \lVert \mathbf y - pX\mathbf w\rVert^2 + p(1-p)\,\lVert \Gamma \mathbf w\rVert^2, \qquad \Gamma = \big(\text{diag}(X^\top X)\big)^{1/2}. \]

Why: for each row \(i\), the prediction \(\sum_j R_{ij}X_{ij}w_j\) has mean \(p\,(X\mathbf w)_i\) and variance \(p(1-p)\sum_j X_{ij}^2 w_j^2\) (Chapter 6). The expected squared error is (mean error)² + variance, and summing the variances over rows gives \(p(1-p)\sum_j w_j^2 \sum_i X_{ij}^2 = p(1-p)\lVert\Gamma\mathbf w\rVert^2\). \(\text{diag}(X^\top X)\) keeps only the diagonal of \(X^\top X\): the sum of squares of each feature’s column, so \(\Gamma\) scales each weight by its feature’s spread. Dropout is therefore, in expectation, ridge regression (Chapter 4) with a per-feature penalty: “If a particular data dimension varies a lot, the regularizer tries to squeeze its weight more.”

∑ The math (paper §9.1, absorbing p)
\[ \underset{\mathbf w}{\text{minimize}}\ \ \lVert \mathbf y - X\tilde{\mathbf w}\rVert^2 + \frac{1-p}{p}\,\lVert \Gamma \tilde{\mathbf w}\rVert^2, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf w} = p\,\mathbf w. \]

Substitute \(\mathbf w = \tilde{\mathbf w}/p\) and divide by \(p^2\). Now the penalty strength \((1-p)/p\) is explicit: 0 at \(p = 1\) (no dropout), 1 at \(p = 0.5\), and growing without bound as \(p \to 0\). And \(\tilde{\mathbf w} = p\mathbf w\) is the test-time weight scaling of Chapter 11 appearing by itself.

For logistic regression and deep networks no exact formula exists. Wang and Manning (2013) observed that a unit’s input, a sum of many randomly kept terms, is close to Gaussian, and trained with that Gaussian instead of sampled masks (fast dropout):

Multiplicative Gaussian noise

Multiplying activations by Gaussian noise with mean 1 works as well as dropping them, perhaps better.

Dropout multiplies each activation by a Bernoulli coin. Nothing forces the multiplier to be 0 or 1. Section 10 tries a bell curve: multiply each hidden activation \(h_i\) by \(r' \sim \mathcal N(1, \sigma^2)\). With mean 1, the expected activation is unchanged, so no weight scaling is needed at test time. With \(\sigma^2 = (1-p)/p\) the Gaussian multiplier has exactly the mean and variance of the inverted Bernoulli multiplier:

∑ The math (paper §10)
\[ h_i + h_i r,\ \ r \sim \mathcal N(0, 1) \;\equiv\; h_i r',\ \ r' \sim \mathcal N(1, 1), \qquad \text{generalised: } r' \sim \mathcal N(1, \sigma^2). \] \[ r_b = \begin{cases} 1/p & \text{with probability } p\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}:\ \ E[r_b] = 1,\ \ \mathrm{Var}[r_b] = \frac{1-p}{p}; \qquad r_g \sim \mathcal N\Big(1, \frac{1-p}{p}\Big):\ \ E[r_g] = 1,\ \ \mathrm{Var}[r_g] = \frac{1-p}{p}. \]

Check \(r_b\): \(E = p\cdot\frac1p = 1\), \(E[r_b^2] = p\cdot\frac{1}{p^2} = \frac1p\), so \(\mathrm{Var} = \frac1p - 1 = \frac{1-p}{p}\). Adding Gaussian noise whose standard deviation equals the activation itself is the same as multiplying by \(\mathcal N(1, 1)\), i.e. \(\sigma = 1\), which matches \(p = 0.5\). \(\sigma\) becomes a hyperparameter “just like \(p\)”.

📄 In the paper

“We recently discovered that multiplying by a random variable drawn from \(\mathcal N(1, 1)\) works just as well, or perhaps better than using Bernoulli noise.” For the same mean and variance, “\(r_g\) has the highest entropy and \(r_b\) has the lowest. Both these extremes work well, although preliminary experimental results shown in Table 10 suggest that the high entropy case might work slightly better.” Table 10: MNIST (2 layers of 1024 units; Bernoulli \(p = 0.5\) hidden, 0.8 input) 1.08 ± 0.04 vs 0.95 ± 0.04; CIFAR-10 (3 conv + 2 fully connected; \(p = (0.9, 0.75, 0.75, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5)\)) 12.6 ± 0.1 vs 12.5 ± 0.1; \(\sigma = \sqrt{(1-p)/p}\) per layer; 10 random seeds Paper §10, Table 10.

A practical guide

Appendix A’s rules: make layers bigger by 1/p, raise the learning rate and momentum, cap weights with max-norm, and keep p between 0.5 and 0.8.

“Neural networks are infamous for requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning. Dropout networks are no exception.” Appendix A collects the authors’ heuristics, each of which you have now met in a chapter:

  • A.1 Network size. Only \(pn\) of a layer’s \(n\) units are present on average, and they cannot co-adapt freely. So if \(n\) units are right for a standard net, a dropout net should have at least \(n/p\) (Chapter 23).
  • A.2 Learning rate and momentum. Dropout’s noisy gradients partly cancel each other, so use 10–100 times the learning rate that is best for a standard net, and momentum around 0.95 to 0.99 instead of the usual 0.9 (Chapter 14).
  • A.3 Max-norm. Large learning rates and momentum can make weights grow very large; cap each unit’s incoming weight vector at \(c\), typically 3 to 4.
  • A.4 Dropout rate. Hidden \(p\) is typically 0.5 to 0.8; for real-valued inputs (image patches, speech frames) input \(p\) is typically 0.8. Small \(p\) needs a big \(n\), which slows training and can underfit; large \(p\) may not drop enough.

Limits and where this leads

Dropout costs training time; marginalising it, normalising activations and later architectures took the idea further.

The conclusion restates the paper in one line: “Standard backpropagation learning builds up brittle co-adaptations that work for the training data but do not generalize to unseen data. Random dropout breaks up these co-adaptations by making the presence of any particular hidden unit unreliable.” It improved networks in object classification, digit recognition, speech recognition, document classification and computational biology, giving state-of-the-art results on SVHN, ImageNet, CIFAR-100 and MNIST. “The central idea of dropout is to take a large model that overfits easily and repeatedly sample and train smaller sub-models from it.”

Limits

  • Slower training. “A dropout network typically takes 2-3 times longer to train than a standard neural network of the same architecture”, because each update trains a different random architecture, so the gradients “are not gradients of the final architecture that will be used at test time”. This noise is also what prevents overfitting: a trade-off Paper §11.
  • Exact marginalisation only for simple models. For linear regression the equivalent regularizer is a modified L2 penalty; “For more complicated models, it is not obvious how to obtain an equivalent regularizer. Speeding up dropout is an interesting direction for future work” Paper §11.
  • Small or huge data. No gain on tiny data sets, less gain on very large ones (Chapter 24, Reuters in Chapter 20).
  • Equal weights, not Bayesian weights. Where data is scarce, a real Bayesian network still wins (Table 8).

Where this leads in the collection

The next lessons pick up threads from this one. Adam (lesson 3) is an optimizer that adapts the step size of every weight, an answer to the paper’s struggle with learning rates and momentum. Batch Normalization (lesson 4) normalises activations inside the network; it also regularizes, and changed how much dropout people needed in conv nets. ResNet (lesson 5) made very deep conv nets trainable. In the Transformer (lesson 8) and BERT (lesson 9), dropout is applied to attention weights and to every sub-layer’s output, a direct descendant of this paper. Word2Vec (lesson 1), the previous lesson, shares this book’s foundations on neurons and gradient descent.

The way one gene’s RNA can be cut and joined in different ways in different tissues; the paper’s genetics task predicts it.
Autoencoder
A network trained to reproduce its input at its output through a hidden layer; its hidden units become features (Fig. 7).
Bag of words
A document represented by how often each vocabulary word occurs, ignoring order (Reuters, §6.3).
Bayesian neural network
A network whose prediction averages over all weight settings, each weighted by its posterior probability given the data (§6.4).
Bernoulli variable
A random number that is 1 with probability p and 0 otherwise.
Classification error
The percentage of test cases whose most probable predicted class is wrong.
Code quality
The splicing score: bits gained over predicting the average distribution (App. B.6).
Co-adaptation
Hidden units that only work in combination with specific other units, for example by fixing each other’s mistakes (§2, §7.1).
Contrastive divergence (CD-1)
The usual way to train RBMs: compare statistics on the data with those on a one-step reconstruction.
Convolutional layer
A layer that applies the same small filter at every position of an image, producing a feature map.
Deep Belief Net (DBN)
A stack of RBMs trained one layer at a time, used to pretrain networks.

A multi-layer Boltzmann machine trained jointly; used for pretraining in Table 2.

Adding transformed copies (shifted, distorted) of training images; not used for the paper’s MNIST, CIFAR or SVHN results.
Dropout
Randomly removing units, with their connections, independently for every training case; using the full network with weights × p at test time.
Dropout RBM
An RBM whose hidden units are gated by Bernoulli(p) variables; a mixture of 2^F RBMs sharing weights (§8).
Early stopping
Stopping training when the error on a validation set stops improving.
Element-wise product
Multiplying two vectors or matrices entry by entry; written ∗ in the paper.
Energy
In an RBM, a number for each joint state; lower energy means higher probability.
Ensemble (model combination)
Averaging the predictions of several models.
Expected value
The long-run average of a random quantity; for Bernoulli(p) it is p.
Fast dropout
Wang and Manning’s method of replacing sampled dropout masks with a Gaussian approximation of their effect (§9.2).
Feature
The input pattern a hidden unit responds to, visible as its incoming weights.
Feature map
The output of one convolutional filter: its response at every image position.
Finetuning
Training a pretrained network further with labels and backpropagation.
Fully connected layer
A layer in which every unit receives every output of the layer below.
Gaussian dropout
Multiplying activations by N(1, σ²) noise instead of Bernoulli gates (§10).
Geometric mean
The K-th root of the product of K numbers; for predictions it must be renormalised to sum to 1.
Global contrast normalization
Subtracting each colour channel’s mean intensity from every image (CIFAR, App. B.3).

1 if the condition in brackets is true, 0 otherwise.
Inverted dropout
Multiplying kept units by 1/p during training so that nothing changes at test time; equivalent to the paper’s weight scaling (§10).
KL divergence
A measure of how far a predicted distribution q is from a target p: Σ p log(p/q); zero only when they are equal.
KL-sparsity
A penalty pushing each hidden unit’s average activation towards a small target value (Table 9).
L1 penalty (lasso)
Adding λ times the sum of absolute weights to the loss; drives some weights to exactly zero.

Adding λ times the sum of squared weights to the loss; shrinks all weights.

Lowering the learning rate during training, e.g. ε₀/(1 + t/T).
Linear regression
Predicting a number as a weighted sum of the inputs, choosing weights that minimise the squared error.

Replacing a random quantity by its average over all possible noise values, here to get dropout’s expected loss (§9).

Keeping the length of each unit’s incoming weight vector at most c by projecting it back when it grows (§5.1).

Keeping only the largest value in each small window of a feature map.

A unit type that outputs the maximum of several linear functions (Goodfellow et al., 2013); best entries in Tables 2–4.

The small random set of training cases used for one gradient step.

Keeping a running velocity of past gradient steps, v ← μv + g, to smooth and speed up descent.

Averaging the predictions of k randomly sampled thinned networks at test time (§7.5).

The sum of exp(−energy) over all states of an RBM; it makes the probabilities add to 1.

A setting in which the model may not use the spatial arrangement of pixels (MNIST, Table 2).

The error rate over predicted phone (speech sound) labels, used for TIMIT.
Posterior probability
How plausible a setting of the weights is after seeing the training data.
Pretraining
Learning initial weights from unlabelled data (RBMs, autoencoders) before supervised finetuning.

A two-layer probabilistic model of binary visible and hidden units with connections only between the layers.

Any technique that reduces overfitting, such as weight penalties, early stopping or dropout.

The probability that a unit is kept by dropout; p = 1 means no dropout.

A code in which only a few units are strongly active for any input.

The sub-network made of the units that survived dropout for one training case.

The fraction of test images whose true label is not among the model’s five most probable labels.

One neuron of a network; dropout drops whole units.

Held-out examples, separate from the test set, used to choose hyperparameters and for early stopping.

The average squared distance of a random quantity from its mean; for Bernoulli(p) it is p(1 − p).

Multiplying a unit’s outgoing weights by p at test time, to approximate averaging all thinned networks.

Using the same weights in many places: across positions in a conv layer, or across all thinned networks in dropout.

Rotating data onto its principal components, rescaling each to unit variance and rotating back, so pixels become uncorrelated.

  • The paper: Nitish Srivastava, Geoffrey Hinton, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, Ruslan Salakhutdinov. Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting. Journal of Machine Learning Research 15(56):1929–1958, 2014 (submitted 11/13, published 6/14; editor Yoshua Bengio). Numbers in this book are quoted from this final JMLR version.
  • The code: nitishsrivastava/deepnet, the first author’s GPU implementation of feed-forward nets, RBMs, DBNs, autoencoders, DBMs and conv nets on cudamat and cuda-convnet, BSD-style licence (Copyright 2012–13 Nitish Srivastava), commit f4e4ff2 (16 Jan 2014). Files referenced: neuralnet.py (dropout forward pass l.186–204, ComputeDown l.207–235), parameter.py (Update with momentum and max-norm l.80–106, schedules l.108–125), relu_layer.py, dbm.py (l.106–115), mc_avg.py, fastdropoutnet.py, examples/ff/model_dropout.pbtxt and train.pbtxt. The paper’s own pointer is http://www.cs.toronto.edu/~nitish/dropout (App. B).
  • Earlier versions of the idea: G. E. Hinton, N. Srivastava, A. Krizhevsky, I. Sutskever, R. Salakhutdinov, Improving neural networks by preventing co-adaptation of feature detectors, arXiv 1207.0580 (2012); N. Srivastava, Improving Neural Networks with Dropout, Master’s thesis, University of Toronto (2013).
  • Key background cited by the paper: Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton, ImageNet classification with deep convolutional neural networks, NIPS 2012; Livnat, Papadimitriou, Pippenger, Feldman, Sex, mixability, and modularity, PNAS 2010; Vincent et al., denoising autoencoders, ICML 2008 and JMLR 2010; Hinton and Salakhutdinov, Reducing the dimensionality of data with neural networks, Science 2006; Hinton, Osindero, Teh, A fast learning algorithm for deep belief nets, 2006; Salakhutdinov and Hinton, Deep Boltzmann machines, AISTATS 2009; Srebro and Shraibman, Rank, trace-norm and max-norm, COLT 2005; Wang and Manning, Fast dropout training, ICML 2013; Wager, Wang, Liang, Dropout training as adaptive regularization, NIPS 2013; van der Maaten et al., Learning with marginalized corrupted features, ICML 2013; Goodfellow et al., Maxout networks, ICML 2013; Xiong, Barash, Frey, Bayesian prediction of tissue-regulated splicing, Bioinformatics 2011; Neal, Bayesian Learning for Neural Networks, 1996.
  • In this collection: previous lesson Word2Vec; next lessons Adam and Batch Normalization; dropout reappears in the Transformer and BERT.

Figures: the paper is published by JMLR under the authors’ copyright with no Creative Commons licence, so no figure is reproduced. Figures 1–3 are redrawn as original interactive SVG; Figures 4 and 7–13 are explored with toy re-runs and, for Figures 9–11, with values read off the plots (marked approximate); the photographs of Figures 5 and 6 are described in words only; Tables 1–10 are retyped as data with citations. What is toy and what is paper: every number cited to a section, table or figure is the paper’s; every number labelled “toy” is computed in your browser (or precomputed with the same code and seeds, in toy-results.js) from the pixel-digit data generated by toy.js.
