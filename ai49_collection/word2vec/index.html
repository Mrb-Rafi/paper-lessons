⚠ About the toy model

The paper trains on billions of words. Here, a toy corpus of about 2,400 generated sentences (about 14,000 words, 120 different words) describes a small world: kings, queens, boys and girls; five countries with their capitals, languages and dishes; four adjectives with their comparative and superlative forms. On it, the book runs the paper’s methods exactly: the Huffman tree, hierarchical softmax, CBOW and skip-gram with the random window, the linearly falling learning rate and the analogy search, ported line by line from the authors’ C code. What is not the paper: the corpus is synthetic and tiny, the vectors have 2–40 numbers instead of hundreds, and every accuracy the toy reports is a toy number. All numbers in the results chapters come from the paper and say so.

⚠ Two papers, one code

Words as numbers

Words as atomic symbols

A computer can store a word as a position in a list, but then no two words are more alike than any other two.

A computer only handles numbers. The simplest way to turn words into numbers is to write every word we will ever meet in one long list, the vocabulary, and to call each word by its position. If the list begins “the, is, of, …”, then “the” is word 0, “is” is word 1, and so on. The number of words in the list is written \(V\).

To feed a word to a mathematical model we usually turn its position into a one-hot vector: a list of \(V\) numbers that are all 0 except a single 1 at the word’s position. The paper calls this 1-of-V coding.

Try a few pairs. The answer never changes: two different one-hot vectors share no position, so multiplying them entry by entry and adding up always gives 0. “King” is exactly as far from “queen” as from “bigger”. The representation says which word it is and nothing about what it means.

📄 In the paper

The paper opens with exactly this complaint: many systems “treat words as atomic units”, as “indices in a vocabulary”, so there is “no notion of similarity between words” Paper §1. The authors admit this choice works: simple models trained on huge data, like N-gram models trained on trillions of words, often beat clever ones. Their goal is to keep that scale and get similarity.

💡 Intuition

A one-hot vector is like a locker number. Locker 41 and locker 42 are neighbours, but that tells you nothing about whether their owners are alike. We want a description of each word, not a locker number.

Words as numbers

Vectors and cosine similarity

Describe each word with a few numbers, and similar words become arrows pointing the same way.

A vector is just an ordered list of numbers, like (3, 1). With two numbers you can draw it as an arrow from the centre of a grid. The number of entries is its dimension. Two measurements of a pair of vectors will matter all through this book: the dot product and the cosine similarity.

∑ The math

For two vectors \(a\) and \(b\) with \(d\) entries each:

\[ a \cdot b = \sum_{i=1}^{d} a_i\, b_i, \qquad \lVert a \rVert = \sqrt{a \cdot a}, \qquad \cos(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert\, \lVert b \rVert}. \]

\(a_i\) is the \(i\)-th entry of \(a\); \(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\) means “add up for \(i = 1, 2, \dots, d\)”. \(\lVert a \rVert\) is the length of the arrow. The cosine divides the lengths out, so it only measures the angle: 1 for the same direction, 0 for a right angle, −1 for opposite directions. Example: \(a = (3, 1)\), \(b = (1, 2)\): \(a\cdot b = 3 + 2 = 5\), \(\lVert a\rVert = \sqrt{10}\), \(\lVert b \rVert = \sqrt 5\), cosine \(= 5/\sqrt{50} \approx 0.71\).

Now suppose we describe each word not by a locker number but by a few features. In the widget below, three hand-picked features (royal, female, young) are enough to make “queen” close to “princess” and “king”, and far from “boy”.

Such a dense list of numbers for a word is called a word vector, word embedding or, in the paper’s words, a distributed representation: the meaning is spread over many numbers, and each number takes part in describing many words. The paper never hand-picks features. The whole point is to learn the numbers from raw text.

📄 In the paper

Similarity is measured with the cosine everywhere in the paper; the analogy test searches “for the word closest to X measured by cosine distance” Paper §4. Distributed representations go back to Hinton, McClelland and Rumelhart (1986), the paper’s reference [10] Paper §1.

Words as numbers

You know a word by its company

Words that appear in similar contexts tend to mean similar things, and that is something we can count.

How could a program discover that “queen” is like “king” without anyone telling it? By looking at the words around them. Both appear after “the”, before “wore the crown”, near “palace”. Linguists call this the distributional hypothesis: a word is characterised by the company it keeps. (This idea is background the paper builds on; it does not state it in these words.)

To make “company” precise, fix a context window: the up to \(C\) words before and after a centre word. Every (centre, context) pair inside the window is one small piece of evidence.

Collect these pairs over a whole corpus and count them, and each word gets a row of counts, one entry per vocabulary word. Rows of words with similar company look alike, so their cosine similarity is high:

💡 Intuition

The count rows already “know” that king and queen are alike. But they are huge (V entries each) and mostly zeros. Word2Vec learns a short, dense vector that keeps the useful part of this information. Methods that compress such count tables, like Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA), existed before; the paper argues neural approaches preserve “linear regularities” much better Paper §2.

word2vec.c reads the text as a stream of words; a newline ends a sentence (the token </s>) and the window never crosses it. The option -window sets \(C\) (default 5). word2vec.c ReadWord

Words as numbers

How a network learns

A model is a function with adjustable numbers; learning nudges those numbers downhill on a measure of error.

Word2Vec learns its vectors the same way any neural network learns. Here are the three pieces, one widget each.

1. A neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds a bias, and passes the sum through an activation function. The weights and biases are the parameters: the numbers that learning will change.

2. A loss is one number that says how wrong the model is. Gradient descent computes the slope of the loss with respect to each parameter (the gradient) and moves every parameter a small step downhill. The step size is the learning rate, written \(\alpha\) in the paper’s code.

∑ The math
\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \, \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} \]

\(\theta\) (theta) is any parameter, \(L\) is the loss, \(\partial L / \partial \theta\) is the slope of the loss when only \(\theta\) changes, and \(\alpha\) is the learning rate. The arrow means “replace by”. When the gradient is computed from one example or a small batch rather than the whole data set, this is stochastic gradient descent (SGD), the method the paper uses for all models Paper §2.

3. Stack neurons in layers and you get a neural network. A hidden layer with a non-linear activation lets the network bend its decision boundary. The gradients of all its weights are computed by backpropagation, the chain rule applied backwards from the loss. Train one:

Remember the hidden layer. It is what makes neural networks powerful, and also, as Chapter 7 will show, what makes the earlier language models slow. The paper’s big move is to throw it away.

Words as numbers

Predicting a word: softmax and cross-entropy

To predict a word, give every vocabulary word a score, turn the scores into probabilities, and punish a low probability on the right word.

Every model in the paper is trained by making it predict a word: the next word, the middle word, or a neighbouring word. Predicting a word is a classification with \(V\) classes. The model outputs one raw score, a logit, per word; the softmax turns them into a probability distribution.

∑ The math
\[ p_i = \frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{V} e^{s_j}}, \qquad L = -\log p_y, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i} = p_i - y_i. \]

\(s_i\) is the score of word \(i\); \(e^{s}\) makes it positive; dividing by the sum over all \(V\) words makes the \(p_i\) add up to 1. \(y\) is the correct word, and the cross-entropy loss \(L\) is minus the log of the probability given to it: 0 when the model is certain and right, large when it is confidently wrong. \(y_i\) is 1 for the correct word and 0 otherwise, so the gradient pushes the correct score up by \(1 - p_y\) and every other score down by \(p_i\).

A model whose score for each class is a weighted sum of its input, followed by softmax, is called a log-linear classifier (the log of each probability is linear in the input, up to a shared constant). That is exactly what the paper’s new models are Paper §3.

⚠ The catch

The sum in the denominator runs over all \(V\) words, and every one of them gets a gradient. With a million-word vocabulary, each training example touches a million output scores. Chapters 8 and 9 show the trick the paper uses to avoid this.

Words as numbers

Language models and N-grams

A language model gives every sentence a probability; counting short word sequences is the classic way, and it cannot generalise.

A language model assigns a probability to every possible sentence, usually by predicting one word at a time from the words before it: \(P(w_1, \dots, w_n) = \prod_t P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1})\), where \(\prod\) means “multiply together”. Language models help speech recognisers and translators pick the most plausible output.

The classic N-gram model looks back only \(N-1\) words and estimates each probability by counting. With \(N = 2\) (a bigram model), \(P(\text{next} \mid \text{prev}) = \text{count(prev, next)} / \text{count(prev)}\).

The weakness shows up immediately: “the queen sells cheese” gets probability 0 because that exact pair never occurred, even though “the woman sells bread” did. Counts of one word teach nothing about another. And the longer the N-gram, the more of them are unseen:

📄 In the paper

N-gram models can be trained “on virtually all available data (trillions of words)”, but for many tasks the relevant data is small: in-domain speech data is “often just millions of words”, and translation corpora for many languages have “a few billions of words or less” Paper §1. Simply scaling counting up then stops helping, and neural language models, which share information between similar words through their vectors, “significantly outperform N-gram models”.

Neural language models before word2vec

The NNLM and the cost of training

The feedforward neural language model learns word vectors as a by-product, but its hidden and output layers make it expensive.

The paper is about efficiency, so it first defines how to count cost. For every model, training cost is proportional to

∑ The math · Eq. 1
\[ O = E \times T \times Q \]

\(E\) is the number of epochs (passes over the data; “common choice is \(E\) = 3–50”), \(T\) the number of words in the training set (“up to one billion”), and \(Q\) the cost of one training example, defined per architecture. Cost here is the number of parameters that must be touched Paper §2, Eq. 1.

The feedforward neural net language model (NNLM) of Bengio et al. (2003) predicts the next word from the \(N\) previous ones. It has four layers:

  • input: the \(N\) previous words, each as a one-hot vector of size \(V\);
  • projection: each word picks its row from a shared \(V \times D\) matrix, its \(D\)-number word vector. The \(N\) vectors placed side by side form the projection layer \(P\) of size \(N \times D\). Because each input is one-hot, this is a cheap table lookup;
  • hidden: \(H\) neurons with a non-linear activation, each connected to all \(N \times D\) projection values;
  • output: a softmax over all \(V\) words.
∑ The math · Eq. 2
\[ Q = N \times D + N \times D \times H + H \times V \]

The three terms are the three layers: looking up \(N\) vectors of size \(D\); connecting \(N \times D\) projection values to \(H\) hidden units; connecting \(H\) hidden units to \(V\) outputs. With the paper’s typical sizes (\(N = 10\), \(P = N\times D\) of 500–2000, \(H\) of 500–1000), the dominant term is \(H \times V\). With hierarchical softmax (next two chapters) \(V\) becomes about \(\log_2 V\), and then “most of the complexity is caused by the term \(N \times D \times H\)” Paper §2.1, Eq. 2.

Here is a tiny NNLM, trained live with the collection’s autograd library. After training, the rows of its projection matrix are word vectors: an NNLM learns them as a by-product of predicting the next word.

📄 In the paper

The authors note that no earlier architecture had been trained on “more than a few hundred of millions of words, with a modest dimensionality of the word vectors between 50–100” Paper §1.1. They also build on Mikolov’s earlier work where word vectors are first learned by a simple model and then used to train the NNLM Paper §1.2, §3.

Neural language models before word2vec

Huffman trees

Give every word a path in a binary tree, with short paths for frequent words.

Picture the vocabulary as the leaves of a binary tree: every inner node has two children, and every word is reached from the root by a sequence of left/right turns. Write left as 0 and right as 1, and each word gets a binary code. In a balanced tree every code has length \(\log_2 V\) (20 for a million words).

A Huffman tree does better when some words are much more common than others. Its construction is a simple loop: take the two nodes with the smallest counts, join them under a new parent whose count is their sum, repeat until one node is left.

Frequent words are merged last, so they sit close to the root with short codes; rare words get long codes. On average this is as short as a code can be: the frequency-weighted average length is close to the entropy of the word distribution, which is \(\log_2\) of the unigram perplexity (the effective number of equally likely words).

📄 In the paper

“Huffman trees assign short binary codes to frequent words”: a balanced tree needs \(\log_2(V)\) outputs, the Huffman tree “only about \(\log_2(\text{Unigram perplexity}(V))\)”, which for a one-million-word vocabulary gives “about two times speedup in evaluation” Paper §2.1. The paper adds that this is not crucial for the NNLM, whose bottleneck is \(N\times D\times H\), but it matters a lot for models without a hidden layer.

⌨ In the code

CreateBinaryTree() sorts nothing at run time: the vocabulary is already sorted by count, so the two smallest nodes are always at the end of the word list (pointer pos1, moving left) or at the start of the new-node list (pos2, moving right). Each word stores its code (0/1 per level) and its point list (the inner nodes on its path). The widget runs a line-by-line port. word2vec.c l.205–270

Neural language models before word2vec

Hierarchical softmax

Replace one decision among V words by about log₂V yes/no decisions along a tree path.

Hierarchical softmax (Morin and Bengio, 2005; Mnih and Hinton, 2009) keeps a vector for every inner node of the tree instead of every output word. To get the probability of a word, walk its path from the root. At each node the model asks a yes/no question: go to child 0 or child 1? The answer’s probability is a sigmoid of a dot product. Multiply the answers along the path.

∑ The math
\[ P(w \mid h) = \prod_{j=1}^{L(w)} \sigma\!\big( (1 - 2\,c_j)\; h \cdot u_{n_j} \big), \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \]

\(h\) is the input vector (the projection), \(L(w)\) the length of word \(w\)’s code, \(n_j\) the \(j\)-th inner node on its path, \(u_{n_j}\) that node’s vector, and \(c_j \in \{0, 1\}\) the \(j\)-th code bit. \((1 - 2c_j)\) is \(+1\) for a 0 bit and \(-1\) for a 1 bit, using \(\sigma(-x) = 1 - \sigma(x)\). Because the two branches at every node have probabilities that add to 1, the probabilities of all leaves add to exactly 1, with no sum over \(V\). This formula is the book’s explicit statement; the paper names the method and its cost, and the code implements exactly this (see below).

⌨ In the code

For every node on the path: f = σ(h·syn1[node]), then g = (1 − code − f) × alpha; the input’s error neu1e += g × syn1[node] and the node vector syn1[node] += g × h. Here 1 − code is the target (“go to child 0” = 1). That one line is the gradient of \(-\log P(w\mid h)\), which Chapter 15 checks against automatic differentiation. word2vec.c l.449–463

Neural language models before word2vec

The recurrent language model

A recurrent network carries a hidden state from word to word, so it needs no fixed context length, but the state update is costly.

The NNLM must choose how many previous words \(N\) to look at. A recurrent neural network (RNN) avoids that choice: it keeps a hidden state \(h_t\), a vector that is updated after every word from the new word and the previous state. The recurrent weight matrix connects the hidden layer to itself “using time-delayed connections”, so the state acts as a short-term memory Paper §2.2.

∑ The math · Eq. 3
\[ h_t = \tanh\!\big(U x_t + W h_{t-1}\big), \qquad Q = H \times H + H \times V \]

The update (standard RNNLM form, not printed in the paper) uses \(x_t\), the current word as one-hot, \(U\) the input weights, and \(W\), an \(H \times H\) matrix. The cost per word, Eq. 3, has two terms: \(H \times H\) for the recurrent matrix and \(H \times V\) for the output. There is no projection layer; the word representations have “the same dimensionality as the hidden layer \(H\)”, and hierarchical softmax reduces \(H\times V\) to \(H \times \log_2 V\), leaving \(H \times H\) as the main cost Paper §2.2, Eq. 3.

After training, step through “the king said that …”: when the model reaches “that”, its memory of “king” makes it expect “he”. Try the queen sentence and it switches to “she”. That memory is what an RNN offers.

📄 In the paper

In Table 3 the RNNLM vectors do well mostly on syntactic questions. The authors explain that “the word vectors in the RNNLM are directly connected to a non-linear hidden layer” Paper §4.3. The RNNLM used for comparison “took about 8 weeks to train on a single CPU”.

The two new models

The big idea: remove the hidden layer

A simpler model that is much cheaper per word can read far more text, and more text beats a cleverer model.

The previous chapters found the bottleneck: once hierarchical softmax tames the output, almost all the cost sits in the non-linear hidden layer (\(N\times D\times H\) for the NNLM, \(H \times H\) for the RNNLM). The paper’s idea is almost blunt: remove the hidden layer. What is left is a log-linear model: look up word vectors, combine them, and feed them straight into a (hierarchical) softmax.

Such a model “might not be able to represent the data as precisely as neural networks”, but it can be trained on much more data Paper §3. And the goal is not a language model at all: it is good word vectors. The paper proposes two ways to arrange this log-linear model, shown in its Figure 1 and the next two chapters:

  • CBOW: predict the middle word from the words around it;
  • Skip-gram: predict the words around from the middle word.

The demo from the cover is the whole story in miniature: a skip-gram model reads the toy corpus and arithmetic on its vectors starts to work.

📄 In the paper

“The main observation from the previous section was that most of the complexity is caused by the non-linear hidden layer in the model” Paper §3. The approach follows the authors’ earlier finding that an NNLM can be trained in two steps, first learning word vectors with a simple model Paper §1.2, §3. The abstract’s headline: high-quality vectors from a 1.6-billion-word data set in less than a day.

The two new models

Continuous bag-of-words (CBOW)

Average the vectors of the surrounding words and use that average to predict the middle word.

The first architecture is the NNLM with two changes: the hidden layer is gone, and the projection is shared by all context words. Instead of placing the \(N\) vectors side by side, it averages them into one vector \(h\) of size \(D\): “all words get projected into the same position” Paper §3.1. Averaging forgets the order of the context words, as a bag of words does; but the words are represented by continuous vectors, hence the name continuous bag-of-words (CBOW). And it uses words from the future as well as the past.

∑ The math · Eq. 4
\[ h = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{c \in \text{context}} v_c, \qquad P(w_t \mid \text{context}) = \text{HS}(h) \] \[ Q = N \times D + D \times \log_2(V) \]

\(v_c\) is the input vector of context word \(c\) and HS is the hierarchical softmax of Chapter 9; \(N\) is the number of context words (8 in the paper’s best setting: four before and four after). Cost: \(N \times D\) to read and average the vectors, then \(D\) per tree node for about \(\log_2 V\) nodes. No \(H\) anywhere Paper §3.1, Eq. 4.

Now watch one training step on the toy, with real numbers. The update is the one from the code: the error collected at the tree nodes is added to every context word’s vector.

⌨ In the code

CBOW branch: sum the context rows of syn0 into neu1 and divide by their count cw; run hierarchical softmax on neu1, collecting the error in neu1e; then add neu1e to each context word’s row of syn0. CBOW is the default (-cbow 1) with starting learning rate 0.05. word2vec.c l.435–494

The two new models

Continuous skip-gram

Use the middle word to predict each surrounding word, sampling nearer words more often.

The second architecture turns CBOW around. Each word is the input of a log-linear classifier that predicts “words within a certain range before and after the current word” Paper §3.2. Each (current word, context word) pair is a separate training example. A wider range gives better vectors but costs more, and since distant words are usually less related, the paper gives them less weight by sampling them less often: for each word it draws a random reach \(R\) between 1 and \(C\) and uses only the \(R\) words on each side.

∑ The math · Eq. 5
\[ Q = C \times (D + D \times \log_2(V)) \]

\(C\) is the maximum distance. Each prediction costs \(D\) to read the input vector and \(D \times \log_2 V\) for the tree path. The paper writes the number of predictions as \(C\) (a word uses \(R\times 2\) classifications, and \(R\) averages \((C+1)/2\), so the expected count is \(C + 1\)). The experiments use \(C = 10\) Paper §3.2, Eq. 5.

⌨ In the code

The random reach: b = next_random % window, and the loop runs from b to window*2 − b, so the reach is window − b, uniform in 1 … window. One detail differs from the paper’s wording: in the skip-gram branch the code uses the context word as input (l1 = last_word) and walks the middle word’s tree path. Over a whole corpus this sees the same pairs with the roles swapped, and it learns equally useful vectors; the book’s toy follows the paper. word2vec.c l.495–543

The two new models

Training the toy, live

Repeat the single step over the whole corpus with a falling learning rate, and structure appears in the vectors.

Training is just the single step of the last two chapters, repeated for every word of the corpus, for a few epochs. Every vector starts as small random numbers (word2vec.c draws each entry uniformly between \(-0.5/D\) and \(+0.5/D\)); the tree-node vectors start at zero. It trains as soon as it scrolls into view. Watch the map: people split by gender in one direction and by royal/common in another, and parallel arrows appear.

The step size is not constant. It starts at \(\alpha_0\) and falls in a straight line to (almost) zero by the end, so early updates move fast and late updates fine-tune:

📄 In the paper

For Tables 2 and 4 the authors “used three training epochs with stochastic gradient descent and backpropagation”, a “starting learning rate 0.025” decreased linearly “so that it approaches zero at the end of the last training epoch” Paper §4.2. Later experiments use one epoch with the same linear decay Paper §4.3.

⚠ Toy versus paper

The toy needs 6 epochs because its corpus is tiny; the paper uses 1–3 epochs over hundreds of millions of words. With D = 2 the toy can still separate gender, but not everything at once: try it and watch the analogy accuracy.

Inside word2vec.c

The released C program is about 700 lines; a handful of them do all the learning.

After the paper was written, the authors released single-machine, multi-threaded code whose training speed is “in the order of billions of words per hour” Paper §7. Its names map onto the paper like this:

Two tricks make it fast. First, the sigmoid comes from a precomputed table, and nodes where \(|h\cdot u| \ge 6\) are skipped, because there the sigmoid is already within 0.0025 of 0 or 1 and the update would be tiny:

Second, the gradient is written by hand in one line, g = (1 − code − f) × alpha. Is that really the derivative of the loss? Check it against automatic differentiation:

∑ The math

For one node with target \(t = 1 - c\) and \(f = \sigma(h\cdot u)\), the loss is \(\ell = -[\,t \log f + (1-t)\log(1-f)\,]\). Its derivative with respect to the dot product is \(\partial \ell / \partial (h\cdot u) = f - t\), so

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial u} = (f - t)\,h, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial h} = (f - t)\,u, \qquad -\alpha\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial u} = \underbrace{(1 - c - f)\,\alpha}_{g}\; h. \]

That is exactly syn1 += g × h and neu1e += g × u.

⌨ In the code

Defaults of version 0.1c (running ./word2vec with no arguments prints them): -size 100 -window 5 -sample 1e-3 -hs 0 -negative 5 -iter 5 -min-count 5 -cbow 1, alpha 0.025 for skip-gram and 0.05 for CBOW. Note -hs 0 -negative 5: by default the released code uses the follow-up paper’s negative sampling, not this paper’s hierarchical softmax; pass -hs 1 -negative 0 to get this paper’s method. word2vec.c main() · InitNet()

It is here because the released code (and most later word2vec software) uses it by default.

Negative sampling drops the tree. For a true (input, output) pair, the model should say “yes, real pair” for the true output word and “no” for \(k\) noise words drawn at random. Each yes/no answer is one sigmoid of a dot product, with a separate output vector per word (syn1neg). Noise words are drawn from the word frequencies raised to the power 0.75, which gives rare words more chances than their raw frequency.

Subsampling randomly discards occurrences of very frequent words (“the”, “of”) before training. They carry little information, and dropping them both speeds training and effectively widens the window for the remaining words.

⌨ In the code

Negative sampling: a table of 10⁸ entries filled in proportion to count0.75 (InitUnigramTable, l.52–68), and the update with label 1 for the true word and 0 for each noise word (l.465–484). Subsampling: a word with count cn is kept if (√(cn/(sample·T)) + 1)·(sample·T)/cn exceeds a random number (l.407–412).

Evidence

Word arithmetic

To answer “a is to b as c is to ?”, compute b − a + c and look for the nearest word.

How do you measure whether word vectors are good? Earlier papers showed lists of nearest neighbours (France is near Italy) and judged them by eye. This paper uses a sharper test, from Mikolov, Yih and Zweig (2013): there are many kinds of similarity, and a relation like big → bigger should look the same as small → smaller. Two word pairs with the same relation form a question: “What is the word that is similar to small in the same sense as biggest is similar to big?” Paper §4

∑ The math · the vector offset method
\[ X = \text{vector}(\text{“biggest”}) - \text{vector}(\text{“big”}) + \text{vector}(\text{“small”}) \] \[ \text{answer} = \arg\max_{w \notin \{\text{big, biggest, small}\}} \cos\big(X, \text{vector}(w)\big) \]

The difference biggest − big is the “make it superlative” direction; adding it to small should land near smallest. \(\arg\max_w\) means “the word \(w\) that makes the cosine largest”. The three question words are excluded from the search Paper §4. The famous example from the introduction: vector(“King”) − vector(“Man”) + vector(“Woman”) is closest to vector(“Queen”) Paper §1.

Why should this work? If the model learned a direction for each feature (gender, royalty, comparative…), then each word is roughly a sum of its features, and the differences between pairs with the same relation are the same arrow. Look at the arrows:

💡 Intuition

Nobody told the model about gender. It noticed that “king” and “queen” appear in the same royal contexts but next to “he” and “she” respectively, and the cheapest way to fit that is one shared “he/she” direction. The paper’s new architectures were designed to “preserve the linear regularities among words” Paper §1.1.

⌨ In the code

word-analogy.c and compute-accuracy.c first scale every vector to length 1, compute vec = M[b] − M[a] + M[c], skip the three input words and keep the best dot products. word-analogy.c l.112–139

Evidence

The Semantic-Syntactic test set

About 19,500 analogy questions of 14 types, scored by exact match.

The paper builds a new test set, the Semantic-Syntactic Word Relationship test set, with five types of semantic questions (about meaning and facts: capitals, currencies, family) and nine types of syntactic ones (about grammar: comparatives, plurals, past tense). Table 1 gives two example pairs per type.

How the questions were made: first a list of similar word pairs was written by hand for each type; then every question joins two pairs. For example, a list of 68 large American cities and their states gave about 2.5K questions by picking two pairs at random. Only single-token words are included, so “New York” is not Paper §4.1.

Scoring is strict: a question counts as correct only if the nearest word is exactly the expected one. Synonyms count as mistakes, so 100% is “likely to be impossible”, especially because the models see no information about word spelling (morphology) Paper §4.1. Evidence

More data or more dimensions?

Accuracy grows with both, but each alone soon stops paying; grow them together.

Training data: a Google News corpus of about 6 billion tokens, with the vocabulary limited to the 1 million most frequent words. To choose settings quickly, the authors first trained CBOW on subsets of the data with the vocabulary restricted to the 30k most frequent words, and evaluated only the questions whose words are all in that 30k Paper §4.2.

Two patterns. Along a row, more data helps a lot at first and then less; for 50 dimensions it flattens near 23%. Down a column, more dimensions help, but 300 → 600 adds little when data is small. Hence the paper’s conclusion: increase dimensionality and data together. And “given Equation 4, increasing amount of training data twice results in about the same increase of computational complexity as increasing vector size twice”, since \(Q \propto D\) and \(O \propto T\) Paper §4.2. The authors also point out that it was popular to train vectors on lots of data but with only 50–100 dimensions, which this table shows is not enough.

📄 In the paper

Training for Table 2 (and Table 4): three epochs, SGD, starting learning rate 0.025 decreasing linearly to zero Paper §4.2, Table 2. Best cell: 600 dimensions, 783M words, 50.4%.

Evidence

Four architectures compared

On the same data, skip-gram wins on meaning, CBOW on grammar, and both beat the older models.

First a fair fight: all four architectures with 640-dimensional vectors on the same data, several LDC corpora with 320M words and an 82K vocabulary. The RNNLM is Mikolov’s earlier model (8 weeks on one CPU); the NNLM was trained with DistBelief using 8 previous words, so its projection layer is 640 × 8. The last column is the syntactic test set of Mikolov, Yih and Zweig (2013), “MSR Word Relatedness” Paper §4.3.

The paper reads it like this: RNNLM vectors are good mostly at syntax; NNLM vectors are much better, which is not surprising since RNNLM vectors are “directly connected to a non-linear hidden layer”; CBOW beats the NNLM on syntax and matches it on semantics; skip-gram is slightly worse than CBOW on syntax “and much better on the semantic part of the test than all the other models” Paper §4.3, Table 3.

Evidence

Epochs, days and DistBelief

Reading twice as much text once beats reading the same text three times, and a cluster makes 1000-dimensional vectors possible.

On one CPU, the CBOW model of Table 4 trained on a subset of Google News “in about a day”, skip-gram in “about three days”. For later experiments the authors used one epoch only, and found that one epoch on twice as much data is as good or better than three epochs on the same data, and a bit faster Paper §4.3, Table 5:

To go bigger, the models were also implemented in DistBelief, Google’s framework for training on many machines. Many replicas of the model each process different data and send their gradient updates to a central parameter server, without waiting for each other: asynchronous mini-batch gradient descent. The step size is set per parameter by Adagrad (Duchi et al., 2011) Paper §2.3.

∑ The math · Adagrad
\[ G_i \leftarrow G_i + g_i^2, \qquad \theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{G_i} + \epsilon}\, g_i \]

\(g_i\) is the gradient for parameter \(i\), \(G_i\) the running sum of its squares, \(\eta\) a base step and \(\epsilon\) a tiny number that avoids division by zero. Parameters with large gradients get smaller steps; rarely-updated parameters (such as vectors of rare words) keep larger ones. This is the standard Adagrad rule; the paper names the method and cites it [7] without writing it out.

📄 In the paper

“We used 50 to 100 model replicas”; CPU-core counts are estimates because the machines were shared, and the overhead of the framework brings CBOW and skip-gram CPU use much closer than on one machine Paper §4.4, Table 6. Best results in the paper: skip-gram 1000d on 6B words, 65.6% total (66.1% semantic); CBOW 1000d, 63.7% (68.9% syntactic).

Evidence

Sentence completion

Skip-gram alone is mediocre at filling blanks, but its mistakes differ from a language model’s, so together they set a new record.

The Microsoft Research Sentence Completion Challenge has 1040 sentences, each missing one word, with five plausible candidates. The paper trained a 640-dimensional skip-gram on the 50M words provided with the task, then scored each candidate sentence “by using the unknown word at the input, and predict all surrounding words in a sentence”; the sentence score is the sum of these predictions Paper §4.5. Try the same recipe on toy questions:

∑ The math
\[ \text{score}(w) = \sum_{j \ne k} \log P(w_j \mid w) \] \[ \text{combined}(w) = \lambda\, \text{score}_{\text{SG}}(w) + (1-\lambda)\, \text{score}_{\text{LM}}(w) \]

\(w\) is a candidate placed at the blank position \(k\), and \(w_j\) are the other words of the sentence. The paper says it used “a weighted combination” of skip-gram and RNNLM scores without giving the weight; \(\lambda\) here is the book’s notation, and the toy uses a bigram model instead of RNNLMs.

Evidence

Examples of learned relationships

The same arithmetic finds capitals, leaders, chemical symbols and famous dishes, and averaging several examples makes it more accurate.

Table 8 shows answers of the best skip-gram vectors (783M words, 300 dimensions) to relations defined by a single example pair, e.g. Paris − France + Italy = Rome Paper §5, Table 8. Notice the honest errors the authors kept: small → larger instead of smaller, uranium → plutonium instead of U.

One example pair is a noisy definition of a relation. The paper suggests giving “more than one example of the relationship”: averaging the offsets of ten pairs improved the best models “by about 10% absolutely” on the test set Paper §5. The toy has only 4–5 pairs per relation and its single-pair accuracy is already high, so the gain there is small and not steady from k = 1 to 2; see for yourself:

The vectors can solve other puzzles too. To pick the word that does not belong in a list, average the list’s vectors and choose the word farthest from the average, a “popular type of problems in certain human intelligence tests” Paper §5.

Evidence

Limits and where this leads

Simple, fast models on huge data give strong word vectors, but one fixed vector per word is not the end of the story.

The conclusion is short: very simple architectures give high-quality word vectors, and because they are so cheap, they can be trained on much more data. With DistBelief the authors expect CBOW and skip-gram to train even on corpora of one trillion words, “several orders of magnitude larger than the best previously published results” Paper §6. Data is the lever; try starving the toy:

What the paper reports beyond the test set: publicly available RNN vectors helped give over 50% increase in Spearman’s rank correlation on SemEval-2012 Task 2 (measuring relational similarity); ongoing work applies the vectors to extending knowledge bases and checking facts, and to machine translation Paper §6. In its follow-up section the authors announce the released C code and more than 1.4 million vectors for named entities trained on more than 100 billion words Paper §7.

Limits

  • No word structure. The models see words as whole tokens and know nothing of their spelling; the paper itself says reaching 100% is likely impossible for this reason and suggests adding morphology, especially for syntactic questions Paper §4.1.
  • One vector per word. “bank” (river) and “bank” (money) share one vector, whatever the sentence. (The book’s observation; the paper does not discuss it.)
  • Order is mostly ignored. CBOW averages its context; skip-gram treats each neighbour separately. Good for vectors, poor for understanding sentences.
  • Strict, single-word evaluation. Exact match punishes synonyms, and multi-word names like “New York” are excluded Paper §4.1; phrases came in the follow-up paper.

Where this leads in the collection

Word vectors became the input layer of almost every language model that followed. Seq2Seq (lesson 6) feeds embeddings into recurrent networks to translate whole sentences; Bahdanau attention (lesson 7) and the Transformer (lesson 8) make each word’s representation depend on its context, fixing the one-vector-per-word limit; BERT (lesson 9) learns contextual vectors by filling in blanks, a descendant of CBOW’s “predict the middle word”. Sentence-BERT (lesson 20) applies the cosine-similarity idea to whole sentences, and CLIP (lesson 26) puts images and words in one vector space. Training tricks continue in Dropout (lesson 2) and Adam (lesson 3), the descendant of Adagrad.

Glossary

1-of-V coding (one-hot vector)
A vector of V numbers, all 0 except a 1 at the word’s position in the vocabulary.
Adagrad
An optimizer that divides each parameter’s step by the square root of the sum of its past squared gradients (Duchi et al., 2011); used in DistBelief.
Asynchronous gradient descent
Many model replicas send updates to a shared parameter server without waiting for each other, so some gradients are slightly stale.
Bag of words
A representation that keeps which words occur but forgets their order.
Bigram
A pair of consecutive words; a bigram model predicts each word from the one before it.
Binary code (Huffman code)
The sequence of 0/1 turns from the root of the tree to a word.
CBOW (continuous bag-of-words)
The paper’s first model: average the context words’ vectors and predict the middle word.
Centre word
The word whose context is being considered (the paper’s “current word” w(t)).
Context window
The up to C words on each side of the centre word.
Distributed representation
A representation in which meaning is spread over many numbers and each number helps describe many words; a word vector.
DistBelief
Google’s framework for training one model on many machines with parameter servers (Dean et al., 2012).
Distributional hypothesis
The idea that words occurring in similar contexts have similar meanings.
Entropy
The average number of bits needed to encode a word drawn from a distribution; log₂ of the perplexity.
Exact match
The paper’s scoring rule: an analogy is correct only if the nearest word is exactly the expected word.
Hierarchical softmax
Computing a word’s probability as a product of binary sigmoid decisions along its path in a tree, instead of a softmax over all V words.
Huffman tree
A binary tree built by repeatedly joining the two least frequent nodes, which gives frequent words short codes.
Language model
A model that assigns probabilities to word sequences, usually by predicting each word from the previous ones.
Log-linear model
A model whose class scores are linear in its input, followed by a softmax; the paper’s models have no hidden layer.
MSR Sentence Completion Challenge
1040 sentences, each with one missing word and five candidate answers.
N-gram model
A language model that predicts a word from the N−1 previous words using counts.
Negative sampling
(Follow-up paper.) Training to tell the true context word from k random noise words, instead of a full or hierarchical softmax.
NNLM
Feedforward neural net language model: projection, non-linear hidden layer and softmax output (Bengio et al., 2003).
Projection layer
The layer that looks up the vectors of the input words from a shared matrix.
Reach R
In skip-gram, the random number of words used on each side, drawn uniformly from 1 to C.
Recurrent neural network (RNN)
A network that updates a hidden state after every input, so it can remember earlier words.
RNNLM
A language model built on a recurrent neural network.
Semantic-Syntactic Word Relationship test set
The paper’s analogy test: 8869 semantic and 10675 syntactic questions in 14 types.
Skip-gram
The paper’s second model: use the centre word to predict each word in its context window.
Subsampling
(Follow-up paper.) Randomly discarding occurrences of very frequent words before training.
Unigram perplexity
2 to the power of the entropy of word frequencies: the effective number of equally likely words.
Vector offset method
Answering a : b :: c : ? with the word nearest to b − a + c.
Word vector (word embedding)
A learned list of numbers representing a word, so similar words get nearby vectors.
